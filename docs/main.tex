\begin{abstract}
High dimensional observational data, such as electronic health records (EHRs), are common in causal inference and comparative effectiveness research. When patient level data are distributed across clinical sites and covariate distributions differ by site, naive pooling or separate site analyses can give unstable or biased estimates for a target population. We propose RoCE, a robust federated causal estimation framework for target average treatment effects with many candidate confounders. RoCE uses one calibration model for the propensity score and density ratio, combines penalized nuisance estimation with cross-fitting to form doubly robust source assisted estimators, and aggregates sources with a standardized Wald penalty. Preliminary simulations with nontransportable sources evaluate RoCE's bias, coverage, and precision relative to target-only and non-adaptive aggregation; the full TATE production study is ongoing. We also apply RoCE to the Right Heart Catheterization study.
\end{abstract}

\noindent{\it Keywords:} causal inference, transfer learning, doubly robust estimation, transportability, observational studies

\section{Introduction}

Real world data, including electronic health records (EHRs), administrative claims, and patient registries, are increasingly used to estimate causal effects in large populations \citep{hernan2016using,sherman2016real}. In practice, these data are often distributed across sites. Patient level records may be held locally because of privacy, governance, or infrastructure constraints, and the number of candidate confounders can be large enough that standard low dimensional methods become unstable \citep{belloni2014inference,chernozhukov2018double,tan2020model}. Multisite observational studies also involve covariate shift across institutions. A source site may improve precision, but it can introduce bias if its population is not transportable to the target site \citep{cole2010generalizing,pearl2014external,dahabreh2019generalizing}.

Several strands of prior work address pieces of this problem. High dimensional causal inference methods combine regularized nuisance estimation with bias correction so that first stage regularization error does not dominate the final effect estimator. Examples include double selection, residual balancing, debiased estimation, and double machine learning procedures \citep{belloni2014inference,athey2018approximate,chernozhukov2018double}, as well as augmented inverse probability weighting and doubly robust estimators based on orthogonal estimating equations \citep{robins1994estimation,bang2005doubly,tsiatis2006semiparametric,tan2020model,smucler2019unifying}. A parallel literature on generalizability and transportability studies when evidence from one population can be used for another after adjustment for observed covariates \citep{cole2010generalizing,pearl2014external,dahabreh2019generalizing}. This concern is central in multisite studies, where borrowing from a source that is not transportable may reduce variance while introducing bias into the target site effect estimate.

Federated learning and privacy preserving distributed analysis provide one approach to this setting: sites exchange model updates or low dimensional summaries rather than individual level records \citep{mcmahan2017communication,kairouz2021advances}. Recent causal work has considered federated hospital quality measurement with target flexibility \citep{han2024privacy}, differentially private multisite aggregation of treatment effect estimates \citep{koga2024differentially}, one shot and multishot federated estimators beyond meta analysis \citep{khellaf2024federated}, federated propensity score aggregation for IPW and AIPW estimation \citep{khellaf2025federated}, and efficient collaborative learning of target ATEs under data sharing constraints \citep{li2026efficient}. Broader federated and data sharing methods address robust site selection, prevailing model inference, and summary-statistic integration under heterogeneous sources \citep{cai2019individual,guo2023robust}, while semiparametric data fusion work characterizes efficiency gains from combining aligned or weakly aligned sources \citep{li2021efficient,li2023weakly}. Closely related high dimensional distributed methods calibrate propensity score and outcome models under covariate shift \citep{wang2024communication,Tong2025disc2o}, while transfer learning and multiply robust federated estimators use related source borrowing ideas to combine information across sites without pooling records \citep{tian2021transfer,Han2023multiply}. Existing approaches, however, do not fully address high dimensional confounding adjustment, target specific transportability, federated information constraints, and adaptive protection against negative transfer in one estimator.

In this paper, we extend Federated Adaptive Causal Estimation (FACE) \citep{han2025federated} to high dimensional settings. RoCE targets the target average treatment effect (TATE) and treats source sites as auxiliary information to be screened and weighted for the target population. The contribution is to combine doubly robust estimation, cross-fitting, calibrated weighting, federated summaries, and FACE-style adaptive aggregation for high dimensional, target specific TATE estimation. Specifically:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Merged calibration for target specific source transport.} RoCE uses one exponential tilting calibration model for each source and treatment arm, combining the source treatment propensity and the source-to-target density ratio.
\item \textbf{Pairwise source assisted estimation under federated constraints.} RoCE derives pairwise source assisted TATE estimators and penalized calibration losses that require source sites to send only summary statistics and fitted nuisance parameters.
\item \textbf{Adaptive aggregation for high dimensional FACE.} RoCE estimates the shared target covariance among target only and source assisted estimators and uses a self-normalized Wald penalty to downweight sources that disagree with the target.
\end{enumerate}

The paper is organized as follows. \cref{sec:notation} introduces the multisite notation, target estimand, calibration weights, and pairwise source assisted estimators. \cref{sec:method} gives the nuisance losses, aggregation objective, federated communication protocol, and tuning rules. \cref{sec:theory} states the regularity conditions and asymptotic results. \cref{sec:simulations} describes the simulation study, \cref{sec:realdata} applies RoCE to the Right Heart Catheterization data, and \cref{sec:discussion} concludes. Proofs and additional details are in the Supplemental Material.

\section{Notation and Setup}\label{sec:notation}

We consider a causal inference problem with one target site ($t$) and multiple source sites ($\mathcal{S} = \{s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_K\}$). The goal is to estimate the target average treatment effect (TATE) using target data and source-site summaries, following the target-population perspective used in generalizability and FACE-style multi-site estimation \citep{cole2010generalizing,dahabreh2019generalizing,han2025federated}.
Throughout, $\E_r[\cdot]$ denotes expectation conditional on site membership $R=r$.
We define the target-site potential outcome means
$\mu^1 \coloneqq \E_t[Y(1)]$ and $\mu^0 \coloneqq \E_t[Y(0)]$, and the TATE $\tau \coloneqq \mu^1-\mu^0$.
For simplicity, we first present the nuisance and pairwise construction for
the treated potential-outcome mean $\mu^1$.  The corresponding objects for
$\mu^0$ are obtained by replacing treatment level $1$ by $0$ and using the
corresponding nuisance parameters.  Each pairwise TATE contrast is formed as
$\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}=\widehat{\mu}_{t,s_j}^1-
\widehat{\mu}_{t,s_j}^0$; the final RoCE estimator aggregates these TATE
contrasts directly with one common source-weight vector, as defined in
\cref{eq:direct_tate_aggregation}.

Each observation contains site membership $R \in \{t,s_1,\ldots,s_K\}$, raw covariates $\boldsymbol{X}$, treatment $A\in\{0,1\}$, and outcome $Y$. We write $\mathcal{D}_t$ and $\mathcal{D}_{s_j}$ for the target and source samples, with sizes $N_t$ and $N_{s_j}$, and $N_{\mathrm{all}}\coloneqq N_t+\sum_{j=1}^K N_{s_j}$. The empirical average over site $r$ is denoted by $\tildeE_r(\cdot)$. To allow flexible high-dimensional modeling, the nuisance models use a fixed working feature map $\phi(\boldsymbol{X})$; one may also read $\phi(\boldsymbol{X})$ simply as the transformed covariate vector used by the analysis. Throughout, $\phi(\cdot)$ denotes this feature map, $g(\cdot)$ the GLM mean (response) function, and $\Psi(\cdot)$ the GLM cumulant; the subscripted $\psi_2$ appears only in the sub-Gaussian Orlicz norm $\|\cdot\|_{\psi_2}$ used in the Supplemental Material. Each nuisance design includes an intercept. In every penalized nuisance-loss display below, the $\ell_1$ term acts only on the non-intercept coordinates, exactly as in the implementation; unqualified vector norms used in assumptions and rate statements retain their usual mathematical meaning.

\begin{assumption}[Identification and overlap]\label{asmp:identification}
For a fixed constant $M>1$ (equivalently, a positivity constant $1/M>0$), assume there exists an informative source subset $\mathcal{S}^*\subseteq\mathcal{S}$ such that, for each treatment arm $a\in\{0,1\}$, each site $r\in\{t\}\cup\mathcal{S}^*$, and each covariate value $\boldsymbol{x}$ in the target support:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{(Consistency)} $Y=Y(A)=A\,Y(1)+(1-A)\,Y(0)$.
\item \emph{(Treatment positivity)} $1/M<P(A=a\mid R=r,\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})<1-1/M$.
\item \emph{(Site positivity)} The target-to-source density ratios needed for transport are bounded on the target support. A sufficient pooled-superpopulation condition is that the relevant site probabilities satisfy $P(R=r\mid \boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x})>1/M$ together with fixed, nonvanishing marginal site probabilities; we do not claim equivalence without those additional marginal-probability conditions.
\item \emph{(Ignorability and conditional-mean transportability)} Treatment is ignorable within each relevant site,
\(
Y(a)\perp\!\!\!\perp A\mid \boldsymbol{X},R=r
\), and every informative source shares the target conditional potential-outcome mean,
\(
\E\{Y(a)\mid \boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x},R=s\}
=\E\{Y(a)\mid \boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x},R=t\}
\)
for each $s\in\mathcal{S}^*$.  We require equality of conditional means, not equality of the full conditional distributions of the potential outcomes across sites.
\end{enumerate}
Sources outside $\mathcal{S}^*$ may violate the transportability part of this assumption; RoCE treats them as candidate auxiliary sources and relies on the adaptive aggregation step to shrink away sources that disagree with the target.
\end{assumption}

\begin{remark}[Interpretation of transportability]
\Cref{asmp:identification}(d) states that, after adjustment for observed covariates, the target site and an unknown informative subset of source sites $\mathcal{S}^*\subseteq\mathcal{S}$ share the relevant potential-outcome regression. This condition justifies source-assisted estimation for the TATE. RoCE does not assume that every source site is informative: sources in $\mathcal{S}\setminus\mathcal{S}^*$ may violate transportability, for example because of unmeasured effect modifiers, site-specific coding practices, or differences in clinical practice patterns. The adaptive aggregation step gives smaller weights to sources whose estimates disagree with the target.

As in transportability and generalizability analyses, \cref{asmp:identification}(d) is not fully testable from the observed data. It should therefore be viewed as an identification condition for the informative sources, rather than as a guaranteed property of all candidate sources. Sensitivity analysis for violations of transportability is an important complement when unmeasured source-target differences are plausible.
\end{remark}

Before specifying the working nuisance models, consider the source-assisted functional. For any candidate regression function $m(\boldsymbol{X})$,
\[
\mu^1
=
\E_t\{Y(1)\}
=
\E_t\{m(\boldsymbol{X})\}
+
\E_t\{Y(1)-m(\boldsymbol{X})\}.
\]
The first term only requires the target covariate distribution. Under \cref{asmp:identification}, the second term can be estimated from any informative source site $s_j\in\mathcal{S}^*$. Writing $f_r$ for the covariate density at site $r$ and $e^1_{s_j}(\boldsymbol{X})=P(A=1\mid R=s_j,\boldsymbol{X})$, the transported residual identity is
\[
\E_t\{Y(1)-m(\boldsymbol{X})\}
=
\E_{s_j}\!\left[
I(A=1)\,
\frac{f_t(\boldsymbol{X})}
{e^1_{s_j}(\boldsymbol{X}) f_{s_j}(\boldsymbol{X})}
\{Y-m(\boldsymbol{X})\}
\right].
\]
This gives a source-assisted functional with a target regression average and a source weighted-residual correction. RoCE estimates the correction with a single calibration weight that combines the source treatment propensity and the source-to-target density ratio.

We model the candidate potential-outcome regression with a generalized linear working model. Let $g$ be a GLM response function with a canonical link, mapping the linear predictor to the conditional mean of the outcome. For compactness, write
\[
    m^a(\boldsymbol{X}; \boldsymbol{\alpha}^a)
    \coloneqq
    g\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T\boldsymbol{\alpha}^a\},
    \qquad
    g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X});\boldsymbol{\alpha}^a)
    \equiv
    g\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T\boldsymbol{\alpha}^a\},
    \quad a \in \{0,1\}.
\]
For each source site $s_j \in \mathcal{S}$ and treatment level $a \in \{0,1\}$, we approximate the ideal transported inverse-probability weight by an \emph{exponential tilting} (log-linear) \emph{working model}, in line with calibrated weighting and density-ratio ideas for high-dimensional treatment-effect estimation \citep{tan2020model,Tong2025disc2o,wang2024communication}:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:weight_def}
w(\boldsymbol{X},A;\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,a})
\coloneqq
I(A=a)\exp\{-\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,a}\},
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,a}$ is the site/treatment calibration parameter; when the treatment arm is clear, we suppress the treatment argument and write $w(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,a})$.
This parametrization gives convex calibration losses and nonnegative weights with a strictly positive exponential tilt among arm-$a$ source observations. We do not interpret the log-linear tilt as a model for $P(R,A\mid \boldsymbol{X})$; the theory only requires the implied weights to satisfy the balance identity below.
For the pairwise DR estimator $\widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j}$, only $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}$ is needed. If the tilt is viewed as a multinomial inverse-odds model over site--treatment categories with the target site as reference, then $\exp\{-\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,a}\}$ has the same target-density-over-source-treatment form as the ideal transported weight, up to a site-size ratio absorbed by the intercept.
Any remaining multiplicative normalizing constant (equivalently, an additive constant in the log-tilt) is likewise absorbed into the intercept component of $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}$. Consequently, when $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}^*$ is the population solution of the calibration/balancing equation, the calibration weight satisfies the \emph{balancing property}: for every integrable function $h$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balancing_property}
    \E_{s_j}\!\bigl[w(\boldsymbol{X}; \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}^*)\,h(\boldsymbol{X})\bigr] = \E_t\!\bigl[h(\boldsymbol{X})\bigr],
\end{equation}
which is the balance identity used by the pairwise doubly robust estimator below. The identity for \emph{all} integrable $h$ corresponds to the ideal population density-ratio relationship. In the high-dimensional analysis, balance is imposed over a finite-dimensional function class, typically the linear span of the chosen features $h(\boldsymbol{X})\in\mathrm{span}\{\phi_1(\boldsymbol{X}),\ldots,\phi_p(\boldsymbol{X})\}$ (including an intercept). The corresponding ``weight-model correctness'' condition in the Supplemental Material is defined relative to this function class.
This inverse-odds interpretation is therefore only a motivation for the merged form; the estimation procedure treats $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,a}$ as a calibration parameter defined by the regularized loss and its induced balance moments.

The doubly robust estimators combine outcome modeling and inverse probability weighting, following the classical AIPW/semiparametric estimating-equation construction \citep{robins1994estimation,bang2005doubly,tsiatis2006semiparametric}. The estimator for $\mu^1$ assisted by source site $s_j$ is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:source_assisted_mu1}
\widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j} = \tildeE_{t}\bigl(g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^1)\bigr) + \tildeE_{s_j}\left(\frac{I(A = 1)}{e^{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1}}} \bigl(Y - g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^1)\bigr)\right)
\end{equation}
The estimator for the control group, $\widehat{\mu}^0_{t,s_j}$, is defined symmetrically by using the nuisance parameters $(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^0,\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,0})$ and replacing $I(A=1)$ by $I(A=0)$ in the weighted residual term:
\begin{equation*}
\widehat{\mu}^0_{t,s_j} = \tildeE_{t}\bigl(g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^0)\bigr) + \tildeE_{s_j}\left(\frac{I(A = 0)}{e^{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,0}}} \bigl(Y - g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^0)\bigr)\right).
\end{equation*}
Finally, the (pairwise) TATE estimator is $\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}\coloneqq \widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j}-\widehat{\mu}^0_{t,s_j}$, and the target-only TATE estimator is $\widehat{\tau}_{ot}\coloneqq \widehat{\mu}^1_{ot}-\widehat{\mu}^0_{ot}$.

The target-only estimator $\widehat{\mu}^1_{ot}$ is a standard AIPW estimator on the target data alone and requires the \emph{target-site propensity score} $\pi_{ot}(\boldsymbol{X}) \coloneqq P(A=1 \mid R=t,\, \boldsymbol{X})$, which models treatment assignment \emph{within} the target population.
This is distinct from the cross-site calibration/tilting parameters $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,a}$ defined above:
$\pi_{ot}$ is estimated locally at the target site (e.g., via $\ell_1$-penalized logistic regression on $(A,\boldsymbol{X})$ pairs from $\mathcal{D}_t$), and does not enter the source-assisted estimator $\widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j}$ or its loss functions.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

Building on \cref{sec:notation}, this section describes how RoCE estimates the nuisance functions, aggregates source assisted estimators, implements the procedure under federated information constraints, and selects tuning parameters.

\subsection{Cross-Calibrated Nuisance Estimation}\label{sec:nuisance}

The source assisted estimator in \cref{eq:source_assisted_mu1} depends on two nuisance components: the transported outcome regression $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^a$ and the merged site/treatment tilting parameter $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,a}$. The calibrated losses estimate these nuisance parameters and make the final source assisted estimator insensitive to first order errors. This follows the calibrated estimation approach of \citet{tan2020model}: the first order conditions are chosen to match the orthogonality moments of the target estimator.

Let $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^{1,*}$ and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}^*$ denote the population targets (probability limits / pseudo-true parameters) associated with the nuisance estimation step; under correct specification of the corresponding nuisance model, these coincide with the true parameters. Let $\mu^{1,*}_{t,s_j}$ be the source-assisted functional obtained by evaluating the pairwise estimator at these population targets. The asymptotic error of $\widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j}$ can be decomposed into terms related to the estimation errors of the parameters $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$:
\begin{equation} \label{eq:if_decomposition}
\widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j} - \mu^{1,*}_{t,s_j}
=
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^1 - \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^{1,*})^T \boldsymbol{U}_{\alpha}
+(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1} - \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}^*)^T \boldsymbol{U}_{\gamma}
+ \text{h.o.t.}
\end{equation}
The two vectors multiplying the nuisance errors are
\begin{equation} \label{eq:score_vectors}
\begin{aligned}
\boldsymbol{U}_{\alpha}
&=
\tildeE_{t} \left[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^{1,*}) \right]
- \tildeE_{s_j} \left[
\frac{I(A = 1)}{e^{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}^*}}
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^{1,*})
\right],\\
\boldsymbol{U}_{\gamma}
&=
\tildeE_{s_j}  \left[
-\frac{I(A = 1)}{e^{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}^*}}
\phi(\boldsymbol{X})
\{Y - g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^{1,*})\}
\right].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
 
The first moment balances the weighted source treatment arm against the target average of the outcome regression derivative. The second makes the weighted source residuals orthogonal to the working features. When these moments are small at the fitted nuisances, the first order terms in \cref{eq:if_decomposition} vanish and the remaining nuisance bias is second order.

We define the nuisance losses from these moments. The $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ loss is calibrated against the outcome model, so its first order condition enforces $\boldsymbol{U}_{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{0}$. The $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ loss is calibrated against the weighting model, so its first order condition enforces $\boldsymbol{U}_{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{0}$.

Let $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{init}}$ and $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{init}}$ denote initial plug-in estimates of the outcome and site/treatment parameters, respectively. To ensure numerical stability and satisfy the positivity regularity conditions used in the theory, define the truncation function $\mathcal{T}(x) \coloneqq \mathrm{sign}(x)\min\{|x|,M_{\tau}\}$, where $M_{\tau}$ may depend on sample size and is chosen sufficiently large so truncation is asymptotically inactive at the population limit. The subscript recalls the TATE parameter $\tau$ defined above, and helps distinguish this numerical threshold from the treatment level.
In the finite-sample implementation, truncation is used both when fitting the calibrated nuisances and when evaluating the final source correction and its influence-function variance. More precisely, a weight displayed below as $I(A=a)\exp\{-\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T\boldsymbol{\gamma}\}$ is implemented as $I(A=a)\exp[-\mathcal{T}_{M_{\tau,\mathrm{inf}}}\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T\boldsymbol{\gamma}\}]$; by default, $M_{\tau,\mathrm{inf}}=M_{\tau}=5$. The asymptotic results allow these radii to depend on sample size and assume that truncation is inactive with probability sufficiently close to one, so the implemented and untruncated estimating equations differ by $o_p(N^{-1/2})$. If clipping remains active with nonnegligible probability, the implementation instead corresponds to a clipped estimating equation, and sensitivity to both radii should be reported.
For exponential-family GLMs with canonical link, write the negative log-likelihood as $-Y\theta+\Psi(\theta)$, where $\theta=\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ is the linear predictor, $g(\theta)=\Psi'(\theta)$ is the conditional mean, and $g'(\theta)$ denotes the derivative with respect to $\theta$.
Let $\lambda_{\gamma}\ge0$ and $\lambda_{\alpha}\ge0$ denote the $\ell_1$ regularization parameters for the site/treatment and outcome calibrated losses, respectively.

The calibrated exponential-tilting loss for $\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma_calibrated_loss}
\begin{aligned}
\ell_{\gamma, \mathrm{cal}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}; \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{init}})
&= \tildeE_t \left[ \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{init}})^T \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1} \right] \\
&\quad + \tildeE_{s_j} \left[ I(A=1) e^{-\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}} g'(\mathcal{T}(\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{init}})) \right] \\
&\quad + \lambda_{\gamma} \|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}\|_1 .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Symmetrically, the calibrated weighted GLM loss for $\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^1$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:alpha_calibrated_loss}
\begin{aligned}
\ell_{\alpha, \mathrm{cal}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^1; \widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{init}})
&= \tildeE_{s_j} \left[ \frac{I(A=1)}{e^{\mathcal{T}(\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{init}})}} \left\{ -Y \cdot (\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^1) + \Psi(\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^1) \right\} \right] \\
&\quad + \lambda_{\alpha} \|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^1\|_1 .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The key check is the same one used in calibrated estimation. Setting aside the $\ell_1$ subdifferential, the first-order condition of \cref{eq:gamma_calibrated_loss} is the sample analogue of $\boldsymbol{U}_{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{0}$ because a canonical-link GLM satisfies $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X});\boldsymbol{\alpha})=g'(\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T\boldsymbol{\alpha})\phi(\boldsymbol{X})$. Similarly, the first-order condition of \cref{eq:alpha_calibrated_loss} is the sample analogue of $\boldsymbol{U}_{\gamma}=\boldsymbol{0}$. Thus the calibrated losses make the nuisance scores in \cref{eq:if_decomposition} vanish at the population level, leaving a second-order error of order $O_p(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*\|\cdot\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^*\|)$ under standard high-dimensional rates \citep{buhlmann2011statistics,negahban2012unified}; see the Supplemental Material for details.

In practice we first obtain initial estimates to supply the plug-ins. Let $\ell_{\mathrm{GLM}}$ be the standard loss for the chosen GLM, for example squared error for linear outcomes or log-loss for binary outcomes. The initial outcome model and the initial site/treatment tilting model are obtained from
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nuisance_initial_losses}
\begin{aligned}
\ell_{\alpha,\mathrm{init}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^{1})
&=
\tildeE_{s_j}\!\left[I(A=1)\ell_{\mathrm{GLM}}\{Y, g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^1)\}\right]
+\lambda_{\alpha} \|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^{1}\|_1,\\
\ell_{\gamma,\mathrm{init}}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1})
&=
\{\tildeE_t[\phi(\boldsymbol{X})]\}^T \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}
+\tildeE_{s_j}\!\left[I(A=1)e^{-\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}}\right]
+\lambda_{\gamma} \|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}\|_1 .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Using these initial estimates, the source site performs calibrated updates in a two-level cross-fitted way, combining the general cross-fitting idea from double/debiased machine learning with the SMMAL-style two-level calibration logic used in semi-supervised ATE estimation \citep{chernozhukov2018double,hou2025efficient}. For an outer evaluation fold $k_1$, an initial nuisance plug-in is trained for each secondary fold $k_2\neq k_1$ using data excluding both $k_1$ and $k_2$. The final calibrated nuisance estimator for fold $k_1$ is then obtained by minimizing the average of the fold-specific calibrated losses over all $k_2\neq k_1$: the $k_2$ loss contribution is evaluated on fold $k_2$ and uses the initial plug-in fitted on the remaining training folds. Thus calibration uses the full outer-training sample $\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{k_1}$ through $K_f-1$ fold-specific loss contributions, while the final evaluation fold $\mathcal{D}_{k_1}$ remains untouched. The plug-in order is intentional in the two-level cross-fitting construction and does not affect first-order asymptotics under the alignment condition in the Supplemental Material.

\subsection{Influence Functions and Adaptive Aggregation}\label{sec:adaptive_aggregation}

Following FACE, RoCE aggregates the TATE contrast itself. Let
$\widehat{\tau}_{ot}=\widehat{\mu}^1_{ot}-\widehat{\mu}^0_{ot}$ be the
target-only estimator and
$\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}=\widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j}-\widehat{\mu}^0_{t,s_j}$
the source-assisted estimator from site $s_j$. We use one common source-weight
vector for both treatment arms:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:direct_tate_aggregation}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{agg}}(\boldsymbol{\eta})
=
\widehat{\tau}_{ot}
+\sum_{j=1}^K\eta_j
\bigl(\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}-\widehat{\tau}_{ot}\bigr)
=
\Bigl(1-\sum_{j=1}^K\eta_j\Bigr)\widehat{\tau}_{ot}
+\sum_{j=1}^K\eta_j\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}.
\end{equation}
Thus $\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{0}$ is exactly the target-only TATE
estimator. Unlike separate arm-wise aggregation, \cref{eq:direct_tate_aggregation}
optimizes the variance of the scientific estimand and therefore includes the
treated--control covariance.

To construct the variance objective, first form the arm-specific cross-fitted
components, for $a\in\{0,1\}$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\varphi}_{ot,i}^{a}
&=
\frac{I(A_i=a)}{\widehat{\pi}_{ot,a}(\boldsymbol{X}_i)}
\{Y_i-\widehat{g}_{ot}^{a}(\boldsymbol{X}_i)\}
+\widehat{g}_{ot}^{a}(\boldsymbol{X}_i)-\widehat{\mu}_{ot}^{a},
&&i\in\mathcal{D}_t,\\
\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j,i}^{a}
&=
\widehat{g}_{t,s_j}^{a}(\boldsymbol{X}_i)
-\tildeE_t\{\widehat{g}_{t,s_j}^{a}(\boldsymbol{X})\},
&&i\in\mathcal{D}_t,\\
\widehat{\xi}_{t,s_j,i}^{a}
&=
w(\boldsymbol{X}_i;\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,a})
\{Y_i-\widehat{g}_{t,s_j}^{a}(\boldsymbol{X}_i)\}
-\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j}^{a},
&&i\in\mathcal{D}_{s_j},
\end{aligned}
\]
where $\widehat{\pi}_{ot,a}(\boldsymbol{X})=
a\widehat{\pi}_{ot}(\boldsymbol{X})+(1-a)\{1-\widehat{\pi}_{ot}(\boldsymbol{X})\}$
and $\widehat{g}_{t,s_j}^{a}(\boldsymbol{X})
=g\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X});\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{a}\}$.
Crucially, RoCE takes the treatment contrast \emph{before} calculating any
variance or covariance:
\[
\widehat{\varphi}_{ot,i}^{\tau}
=\widehat{\varphi}_{ot,i}^{1}-\widehat{\varphi}_{ot,i}^{0},\qquad
\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j,i}^{\tau}
=\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j,i}^{1}-\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j,i}^{0},\qquad
\widehat{\xi}_{t,s_j,i}^{\tau}
=\widehat{\xi}_{t,s_j,i}^{1}-\widehat{\xi}_{t,s_j,i}^{0}.
\]
For example,
$\widehat{\Var}_t(\widehat{\varphi}_{ot}^{\tau})
=\widehat{\Var}_t(\widehat{\varphi}_{ot}^{1})
+\widehat{\Var}_t(\widehat{\varphi}_{ot}^{0})
-2\widehat{\Cov}_t(\widehat{\varphi}_{ot}^{1},
\widehat{\varphi}_{ot}^{0})$, so the cross-arm covariance is retained.

For any site $r$, write $\widehat{\Cov}_r(U,V)$ for the within-site empirical
covariance. Define
\[
\begin{gathered}
\widehat V_{ot}^{\tau}
=\widehat{\Var}_t(\widehat{\varphi}_{ot}^{\tau}),\quad
\widehat V_{t,s_j}^{\tau}
=\widehat{\Var}_t(\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j}^{\tau}),\quad
\widehat V_{s_j}^{\tau}
=\widehat{\Var}_{s_j}(\widehat{\xi}_{t,s_j}^{\tau}),\\
\widehat C_{ot,s_j}^{\tau}
=\widehat{\Cov}_t(\widehat{\varphi}_{ot}^{\tau},
\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j}^{\tau}),\qquad
\widehat C_{t,s_j,s_k}^{\tau}
=\widehat{\Cov}_t(\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j}^{\tau},
\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_k}^{\tau}).
\end{gathered}
\]
The cross-source term is required because every source-assisted TATE reuses the
same target observations, as in FACE \citep{han2025federated}.

In finite samples, the plug-in variance/covariance components (especially the shared-target covariance terms) can yield a quadratic objective in \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective} that is only approximately convex due to estimation noise.
For numerical stability, the estimated covariance inputs may be symmetrized and minimally ridge-regularized when needed to keep the weight optimization well posed; this ridge vanishes asymptotically under the regularity conditions in the Supplemental Material.

The aggregation weights $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}} = (\widehat{\eta}_1, \ldots, \widehat{\eta}_K)$ minimize an estimated asymptotic variance plus a source-selection penalty over $\mathbb R^K$, matching the implementation. Under the positive-definite population curvature condition below, the variance objective is coercive and has a finite unique minimizer. The minimally ridged empirical quadratic is likewise coercive; the resulting weights are $O_p(1)$, so no arbitrary finite-sample radius $C_\eta$ is introduced. The objective is
\begin{equation} \label{eq:agg_penalized_objective}
\begin{aligned}
\min_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb R^K} \quad &
N_{\mathrm{all}}\Biggl[
\frac{1}{N_t}\Bigl(1 - \sum_{j=1}^K \eta_j\Bigr)^2 \widehat{V}_{ot}^{\tau}
+ \sum_{j=1}^K \frac{\eta_j^2}{N_t} \widehat{V}_{t,s_j}^{\tau}
+ \sum_{j=1}^K \frac{\eta_j^2}{N_{s_j}} \widehat{V}_{s_j}^{\tau} \\
&+ \frac{2}{N_t}\sum_{j=1}^K \eta_j\Bigl(1 - \sum_{k=1}^K \eta_k\Bigr)\widehat{C}_{ot,s_j}^{\tau}
+ \frac{2}{N_t}\sum_{j<k} \eta_j \eta_k \widehat{C}_{t,s_j,s_k}^{\tau} \\
&\Biggr]
+ \sum_{j = 1}^K\bigl(\lambda\,\widehat{t}_j^{\tau}-1\bigr)_+\left|\eta_j\right|
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $(x)_+=\max(x,0)$ and
$\mathrm{disc}_j^\tau\coloneqq\widehat{\tau}_{ot}
-\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}$ is the TATE discrepancy. Its Wald statistic is
\[
\widehat{t}_j^\tau
\;=\;
\frac{\bigl|\widehat{\tau}_{ot}-\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}\bigr|}
{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\mathrm{disc}_j^\tau)},
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\mathrm{disc}_j^\tau)^2
\;=\;
\frac{\widehat{V}_{ot}^{\tau}}{N_t}
+\frac{\widehat{V}_{t,s_j}^{\tau}}{N_t}
+\frac{\widehat{V}_{s_j}^{\tau}}{N_{s_j}}
-\frac{2\,\widehat{C}_{ot,s_j}^{\tau}}{N_t}
\;=\;
\widehat{\Var}\bigl(\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}-\widehat{\tau}_{ot}\bigr).
\]
The $\ell_1$ coefficient on $|\eta_j|$ is zero when
$\widehat{t}_j^\tau\le1/\lambda$ and positive otherwise, so $1/\lambda$ is a
TATE-level Wald penalty-activation threshold. Crossing this threshold activates
shrinkage but does not, by itself, guarantee an exactly zero finite-sample
weight.

Because $\widehat{t}_j^\tau$ is self-normalized, the decision to activate a
source's penalty at the cutoff $1/\lambda$ is invariant to a common rescaling
of the outcome. The full finite-sample minimizer need not be invariant to such
a rescaling, because the quadratic variance and the dimensionless penalty then
change relative scale; throughout the simulations and RHC analysis the binary
outcome has its natural probability scale. A TATE-incompatible source
with separation $b_j^\tau>0$ has
$\widehat{t}_j^\tau=\Theta_p(\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{all}}}b_j^\tau)$, whereas a
TATE-compatible source has $\widehat{t}_j^\tau=O_p(1)$.

\begin{remark}[Finite-sample Wald cutoff]\label{rem:finite_sample_wald_cutoff}
The asymptotic oracle-selection proof uses a growing Wald penalty-activation threshold
$1/\lambda_N$. The production implementation uses $c=1/\lambda=1$: source
$s_j$ is unpenalized when $\widehat{t}_j^\tau\le1$ and shrunk toward zero
otherwise. This cutoff was locked by the coverage-blind pilot rule described in
\cref{sec:simulations}, using simulation seeds disjoint from the production
replicates. Sensitivity analyses use the full pre-specified cutoff grid; within
an analyzed data set, the cutoff remains fixed and the weights are estimated
using only the outer-training sample.
Accordingly, the oracle-selection and normal-limit theorem below applies to the
rate-indexed sequence $\lambda_N$, not automatically to the reported fixed
cutoff $c=1$.  Coverage for that finite-sample implementation is assessed by
the Monte Carlo and cutoff-sensitivity analyses; extending the same oracle
limit to a fixed cutoff would require an additional argument.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Relation to the original FACE objective]\label{rem:original_face_objective}
The original FACE adaptive-aggregation objective \citep[Equation~(11)]{han2025federated} uses the raw squared discrepancy as the adaptive penalty factor. In the present notation, its direct RoCE analogue would replace the last term in \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective} by
\[
\lambda_{\mathrm{sq},N}\sum_{j=1}^K |\eta_j|\,
\bigl(\widehat{\tau}_{ot}-\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}\bigr)^2,
\]
where $\lambda_{\mathrm{sq},N}$ is a diverging tuning parameter. We instead use the self-normalized truncated-Wald version in \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective}, following FACE \citep[Remark~9]{han2025federated}, because it puts source discrepancies on a standard-error scale. The Supplemental Material gives the corresponding squared-discrepancy objective and the stronger tuning/separation conditions under which it gives the same oracle selection conclusion.
\end{remark}

For a cross-fitted observation, let $\widehat{\Phi}_i^\tau$ denote the
uncentered, site-scaled pseudo-value induced by
\cref{eq:direct_tate_aggregation}: target observations carry the target-only and
target-side regression components, and observations at source $s_j$ carry
$\widehat{\eta}_j(\widehat{\xi}_{t,s_j,i}^\tau+
\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j}^\tau)$, with factors
$N_{\mathrm{all}}/N_t$ and $N_{\mathrm{all}}/N_{s_j}$, respectively. The
implementation computes the point estimate and variance from these same
pseudo-values:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tate_federated_variance}
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{agg}}
=N_{\mathrm{all}}^{-1}\sum_r\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_r}
\widehat{\Phi}_i^\tau,\qquad
\widehat V_\tau
=N_{\mathrm{all}}^{-1}\sum_r\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_r}
\bigl(\widehat{\Phi}_i^\tau-\overline{\widehat{\Phi}}_r^\tau\bigr)^2.
\end{equation}
Centering is within site, as required by the fixed-site multisample asymptotic
regime. Each site need only return its sample size, pseudo-value sum, and
centered sum of squares. The reported standard error is
$\{\widehat V_\tau/N_{\mathrm{all}}\}^{1/2}$.

\subsection{Federated Algorithm}\label{sec:algorithms}

RoCE can be implemented as a summary-exchange algorithm. Each site keeps its individual-level records locally, while the target and source sites pass only fitted nuisance parameters, empirical moments, sample sizes, and variance summaries \citep{mcmahan2017communication,kairouz2021advances,han2025federated}. This data-locality property is not, by itself, a formal privacy guarantee; differential privacy, secure aggregation, and disclosure-risk control are outside the present scope. The two-level cross-fitting separates the outer evaluation fold from the secondary calibration folds. We consider two communication patterns that differ only in how each source's nuisances are initialized. The default one-round algorithm uses the target-only outcome model as the shared initialization, and each source returns its calibrated summaries in a single round. A two-round version instead uses a first round for source-specific initialization. In an archived continuous-outcome communication diagnostic, the preceding arm-wise TATE implementations of the two variants track one another closely when the sources are mean-compatible for the TATE, even though their conditional outcome regressions differ in shape from the target's (\cref{fig:one_vs_two_round}). This provides descriptive support for the communication-light initialization, but the modest archived Monte Carlo size is not a formal equivalence study and the comparison is not relabeled as a common-weight TATE experiment. Nuisance fitting is arm-specific, but the current estimator contrasts the two arm-specific influence components before aggregation and estimates only one TATE-level weight vector. Below we display the treated-arm nuisance step, with the control arm obtained by replacing $I(A=1)$ by $I(A=0)$. The expanded two-round fold bookkeeping and the linear-outcome specialization are given in \cref{supp:two_level_cf,supp:linear_one_round_cf}.

The following paragraphs describe the one-round algorithm, first for a single training/evaluation split and then for the fold-level implementation used for inference. The expanded two-round variant is given in \cref{supp:expanded_two_round}.

\textbf{Local inputs.} At the beginning, the target site holds $\mathcal{D}_t$ and each source site $s_j$ holds $\mathcal{D}_{s_j}$. All sites agree on the working feature map $\phi(\boldsymbol{X})$, the GLM response function $g$, the fold partition used for two-level cross-fitting, candidate grids for the nuisance penalties $(\lambda_\gamma,\lambda_\alpha)$, and the fixed TATE-level Wald threshold $1/\lambda$. The target fits the target-only nuisance models for both arms and constructs $\widehat{\tau}_{ot}$ and $\widehat{\varphi}_{ot}^{\tau}$.

\textbf{One communication round: target initialization.} The target first fits a target-only initial treated-outcome model on its treated observations:
\[
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{ot}^{1,(0)}
\in
\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
\left\{
\tildeE_{t}\!\left[I(A=1)\ell_{\mathrm{GLM}}\{Y,g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X});\boldsymbol{\alpha})\}\right]
+\lambda_\alpha\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_1
\right\}.
\]
It then evaluates the target-side summaries $\tildeE_t\!\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}g\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X});\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{ot}^{1,(0)}\}\right]$ and $\tildeE_t\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})\}$, and broadcasts $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{ot}^{1,(0)}$ together with these summaries to all source sites. These quantities are low-dimensional or $p$-dimensional summaries determined by the chosen feature map, rather than individual-level observations.

\textbf{Source calibration and return summaries.} After receiving the target summaries, each source solves the two calibrated nuisance problems locally. First, it computes an initial merged site/treatment tilting fit using the initialization loss in \cref{eq:nuisance_initial_losses} with the received target covariate mean. It then estimates the calibrated tilting parameter $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1}$ using the calibrated exponential-tilting loss in \cref{eq:gamma_calibrated_loss}, with the target-only outcome fit $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{ot}^{1,(0)}$ as the shared initial outcome plug-in. Second, using the initial tilting fit as the plug-in weight model, the source estimates the calibrated outcome parameter $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1}$ from \cref{eq:alpha_calibrated_loss}. This ordering is the finite-sample realization of the cross-calibration logic: the $\gamma$ update targets covariate/score balance against the target, and the $\alpha$ update targets a weighted residual equation at the source.

The source then forms the source-side correction and variance summaries
\[
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j}^1
&=
\tildeE_{s_j}\!\left[
w(\boldsymbol{X};\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1})
\{Y-g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X});\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1})\}
\right],\\
\widehat{V}_{s_j}
&=
N_{s_j}^{-1}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_{s_j}}
\left[
w(\boldsymbol{X}_i;\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1})
\{Y_i-g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}_i);\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1})\}
-\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j}^1
\right]^2 .
\end{aligned}
\]
The source repeats this nuisance step for $a=0$, forms the contrasts
$\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j}^{\tau}
=\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j}^{1}-\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j}^{0}$ and
$\widehat{\xi}_{t,s_j}^{\tau}
=\widehat{\xi}_{t,s_j}^{1}-\widehat{\xi}_{t,s_j}^{0}$, and sends the two
outcome-parameter vectors, $\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j}^{\tau}$,
$\widehat V_{s_j}^{\tau}$, and $N_{s_j}$. The site/treatment parameters can
remain local because the target does not need to reconstruct individual
source residuals once the contrast summaries have been computed.

\textbf{Target-side assembly.} With the returned source summaries, the target builds
$\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}=\widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j}
-\widehat{\mu}^0_{t,s_j}$ for every source. It then contrasts the target-side
regression components observation by observation and computes
$\widehat V_{t,s_j}^{\tau}$, $\widehat C_{ot,s_j}^{\tau}$, and
$\widehat C_{t,s_j,s_k}^{\tau}$ locally. These quantities, together with
$\widehat V_{ot}^{\tau}$ and the source-side contrast variances, are exactly the
inputs to \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective}. The target selects one
$\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}$, forms \cref{eq:direct_tate_aggregation}, and
reports the variance in \cref{eq:tate_federated_variance}.

\textbf{Two-level cross-fitted construction.} The description above gives one training/evaluation split. The estimator analyzed in the theory uses a fold-specific, two-level cross-fitted construction following the SMMAL-style calibration construction of \citet{hou2025efficient}. For an outer evaluation fold $k_1$, all nuisance models and aggregation weights are learned only on $\mathcal{T}_{k_1}=\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{k_1}$. Inside $\mathcal{T}_{k_1}$, each secondary fold $k_2\neq k_1$ supplies a calibration fold. In the one-round procedure, the target trains the fold-specific initial outcome plug-in and target summaries on data excluding both $k_1$ and $k_2$, broadcasts those plug-ins and summaries to the sources, and each source contributes the corresponding calibrated loss on its own secondary fold. The calibrated nuisance estimator for evaluating $\mathcal{D}_{k_1}$ is obtained by minimizing the average of these secondary-fold calibrated losses. The aggregation penalty parameter $\lambda$ is fixed at the Wald screening threshold described in \cref{rem:finite_sample_wald_cutoff}. The variance/discrepancy summaries and the final weights $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(-k_1)}$ are computed only inside $\mathcal{T}_{k_1}$, and the weights are applied only to observations in the held-out outer fold. \Cref{supp:two_level_cf} gives the fold-level bookkeeping and the two-round variant, and \cref{supp:linear_one_round_cf} gives the linear-outcome simplification.

\subsection{Cross-Validation and Tuning Parameters}\label{sec:tuning}
The framework involves two types of regularization parameters. The nuisance penalties $(\lambda_\gamma,\lambda_\alpha)$ are selected by cross-validation, while the aggregation cutoff $c=1/\lambda$ controls the finite-sample Wald screening rule. The production simulations use the minimum-validation-loss nuisance rule (\texttt{lambda.min}). In the RHC application, a pre-specified numerical audit first evaluates that rule and switches the complete analysis to the stronger one-standard-error rule (\texttt{lambda.1se}) if any selected nuisance fit reaches the numerical coefficient boundary or otherwise fails the strict fit audit; the failed fit is retained as a named sensitivity result. In the main analysis we fix the aggregation cutoff at the value locked by the disjoint, coverage-blind simulation pilot described in \cref{sec:simulations}; the remaining candidate values are retained as a sensitivity analysis.

For each nuisance model (parameters $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ and $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$), the regularization parameters ($\lambda_\gamma, \lambda_\alpha$) are selected via $K_f$-fold cross-validation, where $K_f$ is a fixed constant. The criterion for selection is the minimization of the corresponding calibrated loss function (\cref{eq:gamma_calibrated_loss} or \cref{eq:alpha_calibrated_loss}) on the validation folds. This is performed locally at each site during the federated step where the model is estimated.

The density-ratio path also includes a finite-sample coercivity safeguard. Let $\boldsymbol m$ denote the target-side linear moment in the relevant exponential-tilting loss, including its intercept coordinate $m_0$. If non-intercept feature $j$ is constant at $c_j$ within a source treatment arm used for fitting, then the direction $(-c_j,\boldsymbol e_j)$ leaves every source linear predictor unchanged. Along this direction the unpenalized linear part has slope $m_j-c_jm_0$; because the intercept is unpenalized, a candidate penalty below $|m_j-c_jm_0|$ can make the empirical loss noncoercive. We therefore lower-bound the local $\lambda_\gamma$ path by the largest such absolute gap, plus a negligible strict numerical margin, and record every activation. This safeguard prevents a sparse fold from driving a coefficient to the solver boundary; it does not manufacture covariate support or replace the overlap assumption. A whole site/arm with a persistent support failure is flagged for exclusion from the primary analysis and retained only as a sensitivity analysis. Under the stated positivity conditions, the probability of an empirically empty fixed-probability cell vanishes, so this floor is asymptotically inactive.

The parameter $\lambda$ in the aggregation objective~\cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective} controls the Wald penalty-activation cutoff $c=1/\lambda$: source $s_j$ is unpenalized when its TATE discrepancy satisfies $\widehat{t}_j^\tau\le c$, and shrunk toward zero when $\widehat{t}_j^\tau>c$. A larger cutoff leaves more sources unpenalized and can reduce variance when source-assisted TATE estimators are compatible with the target, but it can also increase bias from incompatible sources. A smaller cutoff penalizes more sources and generally moves the estimator toward target-only TATE; exact zero weights are an optimization outcome, not a finite-sample consequence of crossing the cutoff alone. The oracle-selection theorem uses an asymptotic sequence $\lambda_N\to 0$ with $\lambda_N\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{all}}}\to\infty$ under fixed TATE separation; equivalently, the cutoff $1/\lambda_N$ grows without bound but slower than $\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{all}}}$.

Because the aggregation penalty acts through the self-normalized statistic $\widehat{t}_j^\tau$ in \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective}, the cutoff is scale-free and does not require a problem-scale-dependent grid. We use the locked default $c=1$ described in \cref{rem:finite_sample_wald_cutoff}. For a new application, selecting $c$ from a grid using the observed application data would instead be part of weight estimation and must use only training-fold summaries.

For an outer evaluation fold $k_1$, let $\mathcal{T}_{k_1}=\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{k_1}$ be the data available for nuisance fitting and weight estimation. All TATE variance, covariance, and discrepancy summaries entering \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective}, and hence $\widehat{t}_j^\tau$, are computed on $\mathcal{T}_{k_1}$; the sample sizes used to studentize that discrepancy are likewise the site-specific sizes in $\mathcal{T}_{k_1}$. Under the balanced-fold convention, replacing every full-site size in $N_{\mathrm{all}}\widehat{\Var}(\boldsymbol\eta)$ by its common outer-training fraction leaves that normalized variance objective unchanged, while it gives the correct training-sample Wald standard error. The common weights $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(-k_1)}$ are then applied only to the outer evaluation fold $\mathcal{D}_{k_1}$. No information from $\mathcal{D}_{k_1}$ is used to choose the cutoff. The optional FACE-style validation criterion $Q_{\mathrm{val},m}(\lambda)$ that would be used if one tuned $\lambda$ over a grid is recorded in the Supplemental Material for completeness.

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}

This section states the main theoretical guarantees in the same order as the method is built: first the conditions under which each target-source estimator is valid, then the aggregation result that protects against negative transfer, and finally the implication for the TATE. Detailed proofs, including empirical-process and selection arguments, are provided in the Supplemental Material. Throughout this section $K$ and $K_f$ are fixed, $N_{\mathrm{all}}\to\infty$, the site fractions $N_t/N_{\mathrm{all}}$ and $N_{s_j}/N_{\mathrm{all}}$ stay bounded away from zero, and the covariate dimension $p$ may grow with sample size. We first record the assumptions needed to read the theorem statements; the Supplemental Material gives sharper versions and proof-level empirical-process conditions.

\begin{assumption}[Sampling, moments, and cross-fitting]\label{asmp:main_sampling}
For fixed constants $M>1$ and $\delta>0$, assume the following conditions hold for the target site and the candidate source sites:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{(Fixed site and fold regime)} The number of source sites $K$ and the number of cross-fitting folds $K_f$ are fixed.
\item \emph{(Independent multisite sampling)} Target and source samples are mutually independent, observations are i.i.d.\ within each site, and the site fractions satisfy $N_t/N_{\mathrm{all}}\ge 1/M$ and $N_{s_j}/N_{\mathrm{all}}\ge 1/M$ asymptotically for every fixed source $s_j$.
\item \emph{(Working-feature tails)} The feature map $\phi(\boldsymbol{X})$ is fixed across sites, includes an intercept, and satisfies $\max_{1\le \ell\le p}\|\phi_\ell(\boldsymbol{X})\|_{\psi_2}\le M$ under each participating site distribution.
\item \emph{(Moment and weight regularity)} The exponential tilting weights and the influence-function components used for pairwise estimation and aggregation have uniformly bounded moments of order $2+\delta$ by $M$. Products of any two influence-function components used in the aggregation covariance estimators are uniformly integrable.
\item \emph{(Fold independence)} All nuisance functions and aggregation weights applied to an evaluation fold are trained using data independent of that fold.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[High dimensional nuisance estimation]\label{asmp:main_nuisance}
For each informative source $s_j$ and treatment arm $a$, the calibrated nuisance estimation step satisfies the following conditions.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{(Sparse nuisance targets)} The calibrated outcome target $\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*_{t,s_j,a}$ and the calibrated site/treatment tilting target $\boldsymbol{\gamma}^*_{s_j,a}$ are sparse. Let $s_{\alpha,j}$ and $s_{\gamma,j}$ denote their sparsities, and let $s_{\mathrm{tot},j}=s_{\alpha,j}+s_{\gamma,j}$.
\item \emph{(Calibrated target uniqueness)} The population calibrated losses have unique local targets; under correct specification of the corresponding nuisance model, these targets coincide with the true outcome regression or the true balancing weight.
\item \emph{(Curvature and initialization alignment)} The population calibrated losses satisfy restricted strong convexity on the relevant sparse cones, and the two-level cross-fitting construction aligns the population limits of the initial nuisance estimators with the final calibrated targets.
\item \emph{(Rate of estimation)} With $N_{t,s_j}=N_t+N_{s_j}$, the calibrated $\ell_1$ nuisance estimators obey
\[
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*\|_2+\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^*\|_2
=
O_p\!\left(\sqrt{s_{\mathrm{tot},j}\log(p)/N_{t,s_j}}\right),
\]
with analogous $\ell_1$ rates of order $s_{\mathrm{tot},j}\sqrt{\log(p)/N_{t,s_j}}$.
\item \emph{(Remainder scaling)} The sparsity scaling required for the first order theory is $s_{\mathrm{tot},j}^2\log^2(p)/N_{t,s_j}\to0$ for each informative source.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

\subsection{Pairwise Source-Assisted Estimation}
For a fixed source site $s_j$ and treatment arm $a\in\{0,1\}$, the calibrated source-assisted estimator has the same first-order structure as an orthogonal doubly robust estimator. The calibration losses are designed so that the derivative of the estimating equation with respect to one nuisance component vanishes when the other nuisance component is evaluated at its calibrated target. As a result, the first-order nuisance error cancels and the remainder is second order, of size
\[
O_p\!\left(\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^*\|_2\,
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}-\boldsymbol{\gamma}^*\|_2\right)
=o_p(N_{t,s_j}^{-1/2})
\]
under \cref{asmp:main_nuisance}(d)--\cref{asmp:main_nuisance}(e). In the theorem below, \cref{asmp:identification} identifies $\mu^a$ for the target and informative sources; \cref{asmp:main_sampling}(a)--\cref{asmp:main_sampling}(b) gives the multisite CLT regime; \cref{asmp:main_sampling}(c)--\cref{asmp:main_sampling}(d) controls the influence function expansion and variance estimation; \cref{asmp:main_sampling}(e) justifies conditioning on training folds; and \cref{asmp:main_nuisance}(a)--\cref{asmp:main_nuisance}(e) supplies the sparse calibrated nuisance rates that make the orthogonal remainder negligible.

\begin{theorem}[Pairwise doubly robust asymptotic normality]\label{thm:main_pairwise_dr_normality}
Under \cref{asmp:identification,asmp:main_sampling,asmp:main_nuisance}, suppose that for source $s_j$ at least one of the two nuisance models is correctly specified for arm $a$: either the calibrated outcome regression equals the target-site conditional mean, or the exponential-tilting weight satisfies the target-source balancing identity for the relevant function class. If $s_{\mathrm{tot},j}^2\log^2(p)/N_{t,s_j}\to0$ and the asymptotic variance $V^a_{t,s_j}$ is positive, then
\[
\sqrt{N_{t,s_j}}\bigl(\widehat{\mu}^a_{t,s_j}-\mu^a\bigr)
\xrightarrow{d}
\mathcal{N}(0,V^a_{t,s_j}).
\]
The plug-in variance estimator based on the cross-fitted influence-function components is consistent for $V^a_{t,s_j}$, the total pairwise asymptotic variance of $\widehat{\mu}^a_{t,s_j}$ (not to be confused with the target-side outcome-regression component $\widehat{V}_{t,s_j}$ entering the aggregation objective in \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective}).
\end{theorem}

The theorem gives the pairwise protection that RoCE needs before aggregation: a source can be useful even if one nuisance model is misspecified, provided the other nuisance model identifies the target mean and the high-dimensional nuisance rates are fast enough. Applying the same statement to $a=1$ and $a=0$ yields the pairwise TATE estimator $\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}=\widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j}-\widehat{\mu}^0_{t,s_j}$, whose variance is obtained from the difference of the two arm-specific influence functions. This arm-wise identification route is a sufficient condition for the TATE expansion below. A source whose two arm-specific probability-limit biases cancel can also be TATE-compatible, but only when the contrast itself satisfies the stated first-order linear expansion about $\tau$; cancellation of probability limits alone is not used as a substitute for that regularity condition.

\subsection{Adaptive Aggregation and Source Selection}
The aggregation step decides how much each source-assisted TATE estimator should
contribute after accounting for both variance and possible bias. Define the
TATE-compatible source set
\[
\mathcal{J}^{\tau,*}
=\{j:\operatorname{plim}\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}=\tau\}.
\]
Let $\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\tau,*}$ minimize the population TATE variance
$L_\tau^*(\boldsymbol{\eta})$ over weights supported on
$\mathcal{J}^{\tau,*}$. This definition is intentionally at the estimand level:
a source can be compatible for the TATE even if equal arm-specific biases cancel,
provided its contrast has the compatible-source linear expansion required below.

\begin{assumption}[Aggregation separation and penalty]\label{asmp:main_aggregation}
Assume:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item \emph{(Global curvature and weight stability)} The population TATE-variance objective is a coercive quadratic on $\mathbb R^K$ with a positive-definite Hessian whose minimum eigenvalue is bounded away from zero. Consequently, its restriction to the oracle subspace supported on $\mathcal{J}^{\tau,*}$ has a finite unique minimizer $\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\tau,*}$; coefficient consistency and the vanishing numerical ridge imply $\max_{k\le K_f}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(-k)}\|_2=O_p(1)$ for the fixed number of outer folds.
\item \emph{(TATE separation)} There is a fixed $b_0^\tau>0$ such that
$|\operatorname{plim}\widehat{\tau}_{t,s_j}-\tau|\ge b_0^\tau$ for every
$j\notin\mathcal{J}^{\tau,*}$.
\item \emph{(Threshold rate)} The aggregation penalty sequence satisfies $\lambda_N\to0$ and $\lambda_N\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{all}}}\to\infty$, equivalently the Wald penalty-activation threshold $1/\lambda_N\to\infty$ but grows slower than $\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{all}}}$ under the fixed-separation condition above.
\item \emph{(Studentization and variance-component consistency)} The TATE variance and covariance components entering \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective} are uniformly consistent on the candidate source set. The asymptotic variance of each studentized TATE source--target discrepancy is bounded away from zero, so $\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\mathrm{disc}_j^\tau)$ is well defined with probability tending to one.
\item \emph{(Compatible-source contrast expansion)} For every
$j\in\mathcal{J}^{\tau,*}$, the source-assisted contrast is asymptotically
linear about $\tau$ with influence function
$\varphi_{t,s_j}^{\tau}=\varphi_{t,s_j}^{1}-\varphi_{t,s_j}^{0}$ and an
$o_p(N_{t,s_j}^{-1/2})$ remainder. This follows from the two arm-wise
pairwise theorems when both arms are identified; for a source admitted only
through cancellation of arm-specific probability-limit biases, it is an
additional TATE-level regularity condition.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

The truncated-Wald penalty
$(\lambda_N\widehat t_j^\tau-1)_+$ uses self-normalized TATE discrepancies.
For an incompatible source,
$\widehat t_j^\tau=\Theta_p(\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{all}}}b_j^\tau)$, so the active
penalty dominates the $O(1)$ variance gradient at zero. For a compatible source,
$\widehat t_j^\tau=O_p(1)$ and the penalty is asymptotically inactive.

\begin{theorem}[RoCE TATE aggregation]\label{thm:main_roce_aggregation}
Suppose \cref{asmp:identification,asmp:main_sampling,asmp:main_nuisance,asmp:main_aggregation}
hold and
$\max_{j\in\mathcal{J}^{\tau,*}}s_{\mathrm{tot},j}^2
\log^2(p)/N_{t,s_j}\to0$. Then
\[
\max_{k\le K_f}
\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(-k)}-\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\tau,*}\|_2=o_p(1),
\qquad
\widehat{\eta}_j^{(-k)}=0\quad
\text{for all }j\notin\mathcal{J}^{\tau,*}\text{ and all }k\le K_f
\]
with probability tending to one, and
\[
\sqrt{N_{\mathrm{all}}}
\bigl(\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{agg}}-\tau\bigr)
\xrightarrow{d}
\mathcal{N}\{0,L_\tau^*(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\tau,*})\}.
\]
Moreover, the site-centered estimator in \cref{eq:tate_federated_variance}
satisfies
$\widehat V_\tau\xrightarrow{p}
L_\tau^*(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\tau,*})$.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Inference for the TATE and Efficiency}
The TATE theorem gives the Wald interval
\[
\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{agg}}
\pm
z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{V}_\tau/N_{\mathrm{all}}},
\]
where $\widehat V_\tau$ is computed from the same contrast pseudo-values as the
point estimator. Because $\boldsymbol{\eta}=\boldsymbol{0}$ is feasible and
equals target-only TATE,
\[
L_\tau^*(\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\tau,*})
\le L_\tau^*(\boldsymbol{0})
=V_{\tau,\mathrm{target}}.
\]
This non-inferiority statement is now a TATE-level result; it does not rely on
separately minimizing the two arm-specific variances. It is an asymptotic
comparison with target-only TATE, not a claim that adaptive RoCE has the
smallest finite-sample RMSE among all non-adaptive estimators when every source
is known to be compatible.

\section{Simulation Study}\label{sec:simulations}

We conduct simulations with one target site and $K$ source sites to evaluate
bias, root mean squared error (RMSE), and $95\%$ confidence-interval coverage
under controlled source heterogeneity and nuisance-model misspecification. The
design follows the FACE simulation structure. Each site contains 1,000
observations. The new TATE experiment uses $p=100$ and
$K\in\{2,4,8\}$; only the first four covariates have nonzero coefficients.
The earlier $p=50$ arm-wise results are retained in the Supplemental Material
as legacy reproducibility results but are not rerun under TATE
aggregation.

Covariates are generated independently by coordinate from site-specific
skew-normal laws,
\[
X_{r\ell}\sim\operatorname{SN}(\kappa,1,\nu_r),\qquad
\kappa=0.125,
\]
where $\nu_t=0$ at the target and the $K$ source values are equally spaced
between $0.2/K$ and $0.2$. Treatment is generated at every site from
\[
\Pr(A=1\mid\boldsymbol X)
=\operatorname{expit}
\{\boldsymbol X^\top\boldsymbol\alpha_1
+(\boldsymbol X^{\circ2})^\top\boldsymbol\alpha_2\},
\]
where the first four entries of $\boldsymbol\alpha_1$ are equally spaced from
$0.5$ to $-0.5$, the first entry of $\boldsymbol\alpha_2$ is $-0.5$, and the
remaining entries are zero.
Before treatment is drawn, the resulting propensity is truncated to
$[0.1,0.9]$. This enforces finite-sample positivity but also means that the
untruncated logistic working equation is not literally exact at the boundary.

For the binary outcome used in the reported experiment, define
\[
q(\boldsymbol X)
=(\boldsymbol X-\kappa\boldsymbol 1)^\top\boldsymbol\beta
+(\boldsymbol X^{\circ2})^\top\boldsymbol\beta,
\qquad
g(\boldsymbol X)
=\frac{q(\boldsymbol X)-\E_t\{q(\boldsymbol X)\}}
{\operatorname{sd}_t\{q(\boldsymbol X)\}},
\]
where the first four entries of $\boldsymbol\beta$ are equally spaced from
$0.4$ to $1.2$. Outcomes satisfy
\[
\Pr\{Y(a)=1\mid\boldsymbol X,R=r\}
=\operatorname{expit}\{g(\boldsymbol X)+\Delta_r a\}.
\]
The target and informative sources use $\Delta_r=1$. In the negative-transfer
experiment below, source $s_1$ instead uses $\Delta_{s_1}=1+\rho$. Thus
$\rho$ is a source-specific log-odds shift; the reported TATE is the target
risk difference, not the log-odds coefficient.

The fitted design matrices vary by configuration. C(1) uses the full quadratic
bases $Z=[X,X^{\circ2}]$ for site/treatment calibration and
$W=[X-\kappa,X^{\circ2}]$ for outcome regression. C(2) retains the full
calibration basis but fits the outcome model using $W=X$. C(3) fits the
site/propensity nuisance using $Z=X$ but retains the correctly specified
quadratic outcome basis. Because the source covariates are skew-normal, the
quadratic exponential-tilting basis in C(1)--C(2) is a close working
approximation to, rather than an exact parametric representation of, the
source-to-target density ratio; the outcome regression is exactly specified in
C(1) and C(3).

The TATE production experiment is designed to use 500 Monte Carlo replicates for each
configuration--$K$--$\rho$ combination. We compare RoCE TATE (the
one-round cross-fitted estimator) with target-only estimation, sample-size
weighting (SS), inverse-variance weighting (IVW), federated doubly robust
aggregation (Federated-DR), and pooled doubly robust estimation (Pooled-DR).
Every method's TATE standard error is calculated from within-site paired
treated-minus-control influence values, so the cross-arm covariance is
retained. RoCE and target-only use the corresponding plug-in influence
variance. For SS, IVW, Federated-DR, and Pooled-DR, the reported standard error
uses 5,000 Rademacher multiplier-bootstrap draws applied to the same paired
site-level influence blocks; the analytic influence variance is retained as a
diagnostic. In a fixed-block, 10-stream implementation benchmark, increasing
the draw count from 2,000 to 5,000 reduced the relative Monte Carlo standard
deviation of the bootstrap standard error from $1.86\%$ to $1.16\%$; the
5,000-draw rule was therefore locked before the production rerun. For
Federated-DR and Pooled-DR, normalized exponential-tilting
density-ratio weights are clipped to $[0.1,10]$ as a numerical safeguard; the
implementation records the pre-clipping range, total clipped fraction, and
largest site-specific clipped fraction in every replicate, and also emits a
warning whenever clipping occurs. We also considered a tilted-AIPW baseline, but its unpenalized
parametric nuisances yield unstable tilting weights in high dimension, so we
omit it from the main comparison.

Before the production run, we locked the aggregation cutoff using a bounded
pilot with C(1), $p=100$, $K=2$, $\rho\in\{0,1,2.5\}$, candidate cutoffs
$c\in\{1,1.5,2,2.5,3\}$, and ten simulation seeds 501--510. These seeds are
disjoint from production seeds 1--500. The rule did not use coverage: first
retain cutoffs whose $\rho=0$ RMSE is no more than $1.05$ times the best pilot
$\rho=0$ RMSE; among those candidates, minimize the worst RMSE at
$\rho\in\{1,2.5\}$, breaking ties by their mean RMSE and then the smaller
cutoff. All five cutoffs passed the $\rho=0$ tolerance, and the rule selected
$c=1$. Because this pilot is deliberately small, we report the full cutoff
sensitivity and Monte Carlo uncertainty rather than interpreting its ranking
as a universal optimum.

All reported simulations use five outer cross-fitting folds, the locked
aggregation cutoff $c=1$ (equivalently, $\lambda=1$), truncation radius
$M_\tau=5$, and 100 candidate values in every initial and calibrated
nuisance-penalty path. Monte Carlo replicate $b$ uses random seed $b$, for
$b=1,\ldots,500$. Within a replicate, deterministic site-, arm-, and
fold-specific random streams are used for nuisance cross-validation; hence
changing the number of workers changes wall-clock time but not the fitted
estimator. The primary nuisance paths use the minimum-validation-loss rule
(\texttt{lambda.min}) for both target and source fits. The calibrated nuisance and aggregation problems are solved by the
package's C++ proximal coordinate-descent routines. Within each nuisance cross-validation
path, a candidate penalty is eligible
only when coordinate descent satisfies its complete-coordinate stopping
criterion and yields a finite validation loss in every inner fold. Candidate
fold fits and penalty values excluded by this rule are recorded separately
from final-fit nonconvergence; a replicate fails explicitly if the entire path
has no eligible penalty. Each attempted CV candidate has a 5,000-sweep budget,
with the last permitted sweep forced to scan all coordinates. Within a fold's
descending penalty path, three consecutive nonconverged candidates trigger a
fail-fast rule that marks the remaining, less-regularized tail ineligible; the
numbers of skipped fold fits and excluded candidates are recorded separately.
The selected nuisance penalty is then refit with a 10,000-sweep budget. On the
locked $p=100$, C(3), $K=4$, $\rho=2.5$ audit task, this rule skipped 17,850
fold fits and reduced elapsed time from 6,559 to 3,516 seconds (1.87-fold),
while all 100 audited primary scientific/final-fit fields and all 97 analogous
sensitivity fields were identical. The only changed legacy fields were the
invalid-candidate counts, because an intentionally skipped fit is represented
by an infinite score; every such increase was bounded by its explicit skip
count.
In the formal MSI arrays, one Slurm job owns one
configuration--$K$--replicate seed and returns all six values of $\rho$. It
fits the $\rho=0$ reference independently; for positive $\rho$, a strict
same-seed validator permits reuse only of the unchanged target, control-arm,
and nondeviated-source components, while the deviated source's treated-outcome
nuisances and the full TATE aggregation are recomputed. This reduces
the 27,000 canonical setting--replication rows to 4,500 Slurm jobs without
changing their task IDs or statistical content. Source-specific fits are parallelized across
$K$ workers and the two arm-specific nuisance pipelines for the reference run
concurrently. For $K=2,4$, each source worker parallelizes the five nuisance-CV
folds over five OpenMP threads, requiring 20 and 40 CPUs. For $K=8$, two CV
threads per source worker require 32 CPUs and give substantially better queue
access than an 80-CPU request. BLAS threading is fixed at one thread per
worker. Before production, an operational $p=100$, C(3), $K=4$, $\rho=2.5$
audit compared the reused fit with a fully independent fit from the same
tested package. All 137 shared non-timing statistical and diagnostic output
fields were identical. The grouped run returned all six values of $\rho$ in
3,666 seconds; relative to the 2,866-second independent endpoint audit task,
the estimated six-independent-run benchmark is 4.69 times slower.
These settings are shared by the simulation driver and the MSI Slurm
manifests. Quality control is performed separately for every
configuration--$K$--$\rho$--method combination.  In addition to bias, RMSE,
and empirical coverage, we report the empirical Monte Carlo standard
deviation, mean estimated standard error, their ratio, the Monte Carlo standard
error of coverage, and the normal-reference coverage implied by the observed
bias and standard-error scale. Because all methods use the same generated
replicate, we also report paired squared-error differences between TATE
RoCE and target-only plus every non-adaptive TATE benchmark, together with
their Monte Carlo standard errors and the fraction of replicates favoring
RoCE. We flag incomplete or nonfinite replicates and verify the RMSE moment
identity before interpreting a coverage departure. We
also record nuisance-fit nonconvergence counts, maximum final-fit iterations,
and the final parameter-update-to-stopping-threshold ratio; a setting with any
unresolved nuisance optimization is reviewed before its coverage is treated as
a property of the estimator. For binary outcomes, the same replicate-level
audit records the minimum site--treatment-arm--outcome cell count and the
number of such cells below eight, so sparse-class warnings can be separated
from estimator behavior. Because the aggregation problem is unconstrained,
we additionally record the largest absolute source weight and flag values above
10 for review instead of silently truncating or resetting them.
Thus a C(3) coverage flag can be distinguished as primarily bias-driven,
standard-error-scale-driven, or simulation noise rather than being diagnosed
from a pooled summary alone.

\paragraph{Negative-transfer robustness.}
To assess robustness to non-transportable sources, we make one source site
progressively non-transportable by shifting its conditional treatment effect
by $\rho$ on the log-odds scale, with
$\rho\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2,2.5\}$, while the remaining $K-1$ sources stay
informative. \Cref{fig:sim_negtransfer} reports the archived treated-mean bias,
RMSE, $95\%$ coverage, and $95\%$ confidence-interval length for C(1) at
$p=100$; the corresponding archived C(2)--C(3) and $p=50$ results are reported
in \cref{supp:simulation_results} and labeled separately. At $\rho=0$, all
sites share the conditional outcome mechanism and treatment log-odds shift.
Non-adaptive pooling can then have smaller finite-sample RMSE when the modest
covariate-shift-induced differences in site-specific marginal effects are
outweighed by its variance reduction, because it pays no adaptation cost. As
$\rho$ increases, RoCE
preserves a substantially more stable bias--coverage tradeoff while shortening
intervals relative to target-only; the non-adaptive aggregators trade their
initial efficiency for increasing negative-transfer bias and coverage loss.
The mild coverage dip near $\rho=0.5$ is the weak-separation regime, where a
small source deviation is difficult to distinguish from sampling noise.
\Cref{tab:sim_summary} reports the corresponding numbers at
$\rho\in\{0,2.5\}$ for $K=4$ under C(1)--C(3).
The table is retained as a reproducibility benchmark for the preceding
arm-specific treated-mean implementation and is not relabeled as TATE.
Recall that C(1) uses quadratic working bases, but the skew-normal
source-to-target density ratio is not exactly log-quadratic. The TATE
rerun uses the same data-generating settings, seeds, and comparison methods
and is reported separately.

\paragraph{Diagnostic for the legacy C(3) TATE contrast.}
The earlier separately weighted TATE contrast under C(3), which is retained in
the archived replicate-level files but is not the treated-mean quantity in
\cref{tab:sim_summary}, exhibits undercoverage that is primarily a finite-sample
centering problem rather than standard-error underestimation.  For example, at
$K=4$ and $\rho=0$, legacy RoCE has bias $-0.0159$, empirical Monte Carlo
standard deviation $0.0175$, mean estimated standard error $0.0192$, and
coverage $0.89$.  Thus its standard error is slightly conservative relative to
the empirical spread, but the non-negligible bias shifts the interval away from
the truth.  At the other archived endpoint, $\rho=2.5$, the corresponding
legacy TATE values are bias $-0.0151$, empirical standard deviation $0.0190$,
mean standard error $0.0207$, and coverage $0.90$.  These endpoint results are
retained explicitly rather than being conflated with the treated-mean entries
in \cref{tab:sim_summary}. With 200 replicates, the binomial Monte Carlo
standard errors of these two coverage estimates are approximately $0.022$ and
$0.021$, respectively; the simulation uncertainty does not erase the visible
centering pattern. The arm decomposition makes this centering
mechanism more explicit:
the legacy RoCE treated-mean bias is $-0.0091$, whereas its control-mean bias
is $+0.0068$, so subtracting the two arms amplifies the TATE bias to $-0.0159$.
The target-only estimator in the same setting already has bias
$-0.0128$ and coverage $0.925$, so most of the centering error is present
before source aggregation.  In C(3), the fitted site/treatment nuisance omits
the true quadratic terms.  Although the outcome working basis contains the
correct quadratic terms, its high-dimensional penalized estimate is not exact
in finite samples; the product of the two nuisance errors therefore leaves a
visible doubly robust remainder.  A focused 500-replicate oracle-remainder
diagnostic at $p=100$, $K=4$, and $\rho=0$ confirms this mechanism in the
target-only TATE anchor.  With both nuisances fitted, its bias is $-0.0178$,
coverage is $0.918$, and the ratio of mean standard error to empirical standard
deviation is $1.039$.  Replacing only the outcome regression by its oracle
truth reduces the bias to $-0.0021$ and gives coverage $0.952$; replacing only
the propensity by its oracle truth gives bias $-0.0018$ and coverage $0.952$;
using both oracle nuisances gives bias $-0.0019$ and coverage $0.950$.
At the replicate level, the inclusion--exclusion contrast
``fitted/fitted minus oracle-outcome minus oracle-propensity plus
oracle/oracle'' has mean $-0.0159$ (Monte Carlo SE $0.0003$), directly
quantifying the joint second-order nuisance remainder.
Changing the target propensity and outcome fits to the stronger
one-standard-error rule does not repair this remainder.  In a paired rerun
using the same 500 datasets and folds, fitted/fitted bias becomes $-0.0251$,
RMSE becomes $0.0384$, and coverage falls to $0.872$; the paired estimate
shift relative to the minimum-loss rule is $-0.00723$ (Monte Carlo SE
$0.00024$).  The corresponding interaction remainder is $-0.0231$, versus
$-0.0159$ under the minimum-loss rule, with a paired difference of $-0.00721$
(Monte Carlo SE $0.00017$).  The double-oracle estimates and standard errors
are identical in every replicate across the two runs, verifying that this
comparison changes only the fitted target nuisance rule.  We therefore retain
the minimum-loss nuisance rule rather than choosing regularization to improve
the observed coverage.
Consequently, the C(3) target-anchor undercoverage is primarily the
finite-sample product remainder, not an underestimated standard error.  The
aggregation cutoff and the FACE source-nuisance path do not alter this
independently fitted target anchor, so changing either one alone cannot be
interpreted as removing the diagnosed remainder. The
full TATE rerun applies the per-setting diagnostic protocol above to
determine how common-weight source aggregation changes this anchor pattern.

The same finite-sample decomposition explains why RoCE is not the RMSE
minimum at $\rho=0$. For the legacy treated mean at $K=4$ under C(3), RoCE
reduces RMSE from $0.0248$ for target-only to $0.0160$, but SS and IVW attain
$0.0115$ and $0.0118$. At this endpoint the sites share the conditional outcome
mechanism and treatment shift, and the modest covariate-shift-induced marginal
mean differences are smaller than the variance reduction achieved by those
non-adaptive rules. They use all sites with no source-selection or fold-specific
weight-estimation cost. RoCE remains target-anchored and estimates adaptive
weights from noisy training-fold discrepancies; together with the finite-sample
nuisance bias, that protection costs RMSE in the no-negative-transfer endpoint. The
TATE-level oracle inequality therefore compares asymptotic variance with
target-only TATE and does not imply minimum finite-sample RMSE among estimators
that can exploit the common conditional mechanism without estimating a
source-selection rule.
This interpretation is also visible directly in the binary-outcome DGP. With
$p=100$, $K=4$, and the common conditional log-odds shift equal to one, the
target marginal risk difference is $0.2063$, whereas the four source-law risk
differences are $0.2037$, $0.2014$, $0.1993$, and $0.1970$; the largest
source--target difference is $0.0093$ (integration Monte Carlo SE below
$9\times10^{-5}$).  On the arm scale, the four source-law treated means exceed
the target $\mu^1=0.6927$ by $0.0079$, $0.0153$, $0.0218$, and $0.0292$;
the corresponding control-mean differences are $0.0105$, $0.0202$, $0.0288$,
and $0.0384$. Their partial cancellation produces the smaller TATE
differences above. Thus $\rho=0$ removes conditional-effect heterogeneity but,
because of non-collapsibility and covariate shift, does not make the raw
site-specific marginal risk differences identical. The complete DGP-level
calculation is reported in \cref{tab:rho0_site_tate}.

\section{Real-Data Application}\label{sec:realdata}
We apply RoCE to the Right Heart Catheterization (RHC) study from the
SUPPORT investigation \citep{connors1996effectiveness}, using the covariate set
of \citet{hirano2001estimation}. The treatment $A=1$ indicates right heart
catheterization within the first 24 hours of admission, and the outcome is
30-day all-cause mortality. Insurance classes are used to emulate federated
sites with different case mixes; they are not distinct data-holding hospitals.
The support-screened primary analysis retains a privately insured target and
three sources (Medicare, Private \& Medicare, and Medicaid), and excludes the
small No insurance stratum and the Medicare \& Medicaid stratum implicated by
the same sparse-support component. It contains $n=5{,}039$ patients and $p=61$
full-rank covariates before the intercept: 22 continuous variables, 22 binary
indicators, and 17 reference-coded categorical indicators.

The working columns are centered and scaled using pooled first and second
covariate moments, which are federated-computable summaries; no outcome or
individual-level record is pooled for this preprocessing. The reported
analysis otherwise uses the identity feature map, so the outcome and
site/treatment nuisance models have the same covariate dimension. This
data-local workflow is not a formal privacy guarantee. The primary estimand is
the target average treatment effect
$\tau=\E_t\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}$, estimated with ten cross-fitting folds, 100 nuisance
penalty candidates, the locked Wald cutoff $c=1$, and truncation radius
$M_\tau=5$. The four comparison estimators use 5,000 paired multiplier-bootstrap
draws. The initial minimum-loss nuisance run triggered the pre-specified
numerical audit described above, so the primary RHC analysis propagates the
one-standard-error rule through both target and source nuisance fits.

\Cref{fig:rhc_direct_tate} reports the primary TATE results. RoCE
estimates a $0.0315$ increase in 30-day mortality (SE $0.0194$; $95\%$ CI,
$-0.0066$ to $0.0696$) under the stated observational identification
assumptions. The target-only estimate is $0.0261$ (SE $0.0213$; $95\%$ CI,
$-0.0156$ to $0.0678$). Thus RoCE's standard error is $8.7\%$ smaller than
target-only, a modest precision gain. The non-adaptive SS and IVW estimates
are $0.0604$ and $0.0590$, respectively.
Federated-DR estimates $0.0365$ (SE $0.0215$), and Pooled-DR estimates $0.0628$
(SE $0.0276$). These differences should not be read as randomized comparisons
of estimators or as evidence of a privacy guarantee.

The common RoCE source weights are $(0.089,0.078,0.108)$, leaving target
anchor weight $0.725$. As shown in \cref{tab:rhc_direct_sources}, the Medicare
source has mean/max foldwise Wald statistics $2.37/2.99$ and activates the
penalty in all ten outer-training folds. The corresponding activation
fractions for the Private \& Medicare and Medicaid sources are $0$ and $0.10$.
The conclusion is stable over the prespecified cutoff grid:
$c\in\{1,1.5,2,2.5,3\}$ gives estimates from $0.0315$ to $0.0367$ and standard
errors from $0.01935$ to $0.01943$; every interval includes zero. The $c=1$
row reproduces the independently committed primary fit exactly.

All 660 recorded primary nuisance fits converged, every recorded line-search
failure count is zero, and the largest absolute density-ratio and outcome
coefficients are $2.22$ and $1.34$, respectively, far below the hard bound of
100. A separate audit of all 540 initial density-ratio fits found no
empirically unbounded zero-variance direction and no coefficient near the
numerical boundary. The support-floor safeguard was activated for 59 initial
and 52 calibrated density-ratio fits with fold-constant indicators. Only 1 of
3,341 source correction observations ($0.03\%$) required inference-logit
truncation, and no inference safety clipping occurred. The TATE
pseudo-value, within-site variance, cross-arm covariance, bootstrap, and
provenance identities all pass their strict audits.

For transparency, the minimum-loss nuisance run is retained but is not used
for primary inference. At $c=1$ it gives RoCE $0.0489$ (SE $0.0221$;
$95\%$ CI, $0.0056$ to $0.0922$) and target-only $0.0392$ (SE $0.0239$).
Its control-arm outer fold 4, Medicaid-source inner fold 1 initial
density-ratio fit places the coefficient of \texttt{incomeUnder..11k} at the
hard numerical boundary 100; the ensuing calibrated density-ratio fit records
31 recovered line-search failures. This initial nuisance also enters the
inner-fold variance calculation and calibrated outcome plug-in, so the strict
audit correctly rejects the run even though a finite final estimate is
returned. Reporting both runs avoids selecting regularization because of the
observed confidence interval while preventing a boundary-contaminated fit from
being presented as the primary analysis.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

RoCE is intended for settings in which source sites may improve estimation of a target-site causal effect but cannot share individual-level records. The method combines federated calibration, doubly robust estimation, and adaptive aggregation. The calibration losses enforce orthogonality equations, the aggregation objective accounts for covariance induced by shared target data, and the penalty downweights sources whose source-assisted estimates disagree with the target-only estimate.

The simulations support these conclusions. When one source is made
progressively non-transportable, RoCE maintains a more stable
bias--coverage tradeoff across the source-deviation range and source counts
$K\in\{2,4,8\}$ at $p=100$. Non-adaptive pooling can have
the smallest RMSE at $\rho=0$, but its bias grows and its coverage deteriorates
as the source deviates. On the Right Heart Catheterization data, RoCE
remains close to the target-only estimate and gives a modest $8.7\%$ reduction
in standard error relative to target-only; it is not more precise than every
doubly robust comparator. In the archived arm-wise communication diagnostic,
the one-round estimator performs similarly to the two-round variant when
sources are mean-compatible for the TATE. This is descriptive support for the
one-round default, not a TATE equivalence result.

The theory treats a fixed number of source sites and relies on standard high-dimensional nuisance rates, overlap/moment conditions, and a separation condition for exact source selection. Under these assumptions, RoCE can gain efficiency from transportable sources, retains the doubly robust property for each pairwise estimator, and moves toward the target-only estimator when source discrepancies are large. Extensions to a growing number of sources, weaker local alternatives, and more flexible nuisance learners require additional concentration and empirical-process arguments.

\section{One-Round Communication Algorithms for GLM Outcome Models}

To reduce communication costs, we can devise one-round algorithms. The key idea is to use an initial model estimated on the target site to bootstrap the cross-calibration process at the source sites, removing the need for an initial round of communication from the sources to the target.

This one-round version---adopted as the default in the main manuscript---uses the target-only outcome model, $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{ot}^{1}$, as the shared initial estimate for the cross-calibration procedure, removing the first communication round. It requires the target-site initialization to be sufficiently aligned with the source-specific calibrated targets, a condition the two-round version avoids by re-initializing each source. In the modest archived arm-wise TATE diagnostic described above, the two variants track one another closely when the sources are mean-compatible for the TATE, despite source-specific differences in the shapes of their conditional outcome regressions; this descriptive comparison is not a formal equivalence result for the current common-weight TATE estimator.

\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Round 1: Single Communication Round}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Target site $t$ computes and sends:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Target-only outcome model parameters $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{ot}^{1}$ by minimizing a standard regularized GLM loss on $\mathcal{D}_t$.
            \item The summary statistic $\tildeE_t(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{ot}^{1}))$.
            \item The summary statistic $\tildeE_t[\phi(\boldsymbol{X})]$ for the initial site/treatment model in \cref{eq:nuisance_initial_losses}.
            \item The target site sends all quantities to all source sites $s_j$.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Source sites $s_j$ receive summaries, compute locally, and return:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Using the received $\tildeE_t(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} g(\cdot))$, $\tildeE_t[\phi(\boldsymbol{X})]$, and $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{ot}^{1}$, each source site first computes an initial site/treatment model $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{init},s_j,1}$ via \cref{eq:nuisance_initial_losses}, then computes the calibrated $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1}$ via \cref{eq:gamma_calibrated_loss}.
            \item It then computes its final outcome model $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1}$ by minimizing the calibrated loss in \cref{eq:alpha_calibrated_loss} using the initial site/treatment model $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{init},s_j,1}$ as plug-in.
            \item It computes the correction term $\widehat{\delta}_{t, s_j}^1$ and source variance $\widehat{V}_{s_j}$.
            \item Each source site $s_j$ sends back to the target: $\widehat{\delta}_{t, s_j}^1$, $N_{s_j}$, $\widehat{V}_{s_j}$, and the final outcome model parameters $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1}$.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Target site $t$ receives summaries and performs final aggregation:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item The target computes its target-only estimates $\widehat{\mu}^1_{ot}$ and $\widehat{V}_{ot}$.
            \item For each source site $s_j$, using the received $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1}$ and $\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j}^1$, it assembles the final estimator $\widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j}$.
            \item It then computes all necessary target-side variance components: $\widehat{V}_{t,s_j}$, $\widehat{C}_{ot,s_j}$, and the cross-site covariances $\widehat{C}_{t,s_j,s_k}$ for all pairs $j \neq k$.
            \item Repeat the nuisance and influence-summary construction for $a=0$, contrast the two arms, and solve \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective} once to obtain the common TATE weights $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}$ and final estimate $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{agg}}$.
        \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithmic}

\section{Fold-Level Details for Two-Level Cross-Fitting}\label{supp:two_level_cf}

The main Method section describes the RoCE estimator with two-level cross-fitting in its one-round form. This appendix is not a separate robustness add-on; its purpose is to spell out the fold-level bookkeeping and to record the expanded two-round variant that can be used when source-specific initialization is preferred.

The two-level construction adapts the cross-fitting strategy of \citet{hou2025efficient}. It uses a nested sample-splitting scheme to ensure that the data used to train an initial nuisance plug-in for an observation is disjoint from the data used for its calibrated loss contribution. As in the SMMAL calibration construction, calibration is carried out over all secondary folds rather than over one held-out fold: for each outer fold $k_1$, every $k_2\neq k_1$ supplies one calibration-fold loss, the corresponding initial fits use the out-of-two-folds data $\mathcal{D}\setminus(\mathcal{D}_{k_1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{k_2})$, and a single calibrated nuisance estimator is obtained by minimizing the average of these fold-specific losses. This separation mitigates overfitting bias in the sequential nuisance updates and supports the Neyman-orthogonal expansion used in the theory.

\subsection{Calibrated Loss Functions}\label{supp:calibrated_losses}

The core of the two-level cross-fitting method is the use of specially designed "calibrated" loss functions for refining the nuisance models. These losses are constructed to make the final TATE estimator insensitive to small estimation errors in the nuisance parameters. Following the principles in \citep{smucler2019unifying} and \citep{tan2020model}, we introduce a calibration step after obtaining robust initial estimates.

The truncation function, GLM notation, and calibrated losses are defined earlier in \cref{eq:gamma_calibrated_loss,eq:alpha_calibrated_loss}. The same loss definitions are used foldwise in the two-level cross-fitting construction below.
For a fixed outer fold $k_1$, let $\ell_{\gamma,\mathrm{cal}}^{(k_2)}$ and $\ell_{\alpha,\mathrm{cal}}^{(k_2)}$ denote the fold-specific versions of \cref{eq:gamma_calibrated_loss,eq:alpha_calibrated_loss}, with $\tildeE_t$ and $\tildeE_{s_j}$ replaced by $\tildeE_{t,k_2}$ and $\tildeE_{s_j,k_2}$ and with plug-in initial estimators trained on $\mathcal{D}\setminus(\mathcal{D}_{k_1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{k_2})$. The calibrated nuisance estimators for evaluating fold $k_1$ are
\[
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1}^{(-k_1)}
=
\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
\frac{1}{K_f-1}\sum_{k_2\neq k_1}
\ell_{\gamma,\mathrm{cal}}^{(k_2)}
\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma};\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{init},(k_1,k_2)}^1\right),
\]
and
\[
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,(-k_1)}
=
\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
\frac{1}{K_f-1}\sum_{k_2\neq k_1}
\ell_{\alpha,\mathrm{cal}}^{(k_2)}
\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha};\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{init},(k_1,k_2)}\right).
\]
Thus the secondary folds enter as loss contributions in one calibrated optimization problem, rather than as separate calibrated parameter estimates to be averaged.
In the two-level algorithm, the calibrated $\gamma$-loss uses $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\text{init}}$ while the calibrated $\alpha$-loss uses $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\text{init}}$. In the Supplemental Material, we assume these initial estimators converge to the same population nuisance targets as the final calibrated estimators, so this plug-in order does not change the first-order asymptotic results.

\subsection{Expanded Two-Round Variant with Two-Level Cross-Fitting}\label{supp:expanded_two_round}
This section records the expanded fold-level version of the two-round construction. It makes explicit which observations are used for initialization, calibration, aggregation-weight estimation, and final evaluation. We describe the treated mean $\mu^1$; the control mean is obtained by replacing $I(A=1)$ with $I(A=0)$ and using the corresponding nuisance parameters.

\paragraph{Outer and secondary folds.}
Each site $R\in\{t,s_1,\ldots,s_K\}$ is partitioned into $K_f$ folds $\{\mathcal{D}_{R,k}\}_{k=1}^{K_f}$ with aligned fold labels across sites. Fix an outer evaluation fold $k_1$. The held-out data $\mathcal{D}_{R,k_1}$ are used only for evaluating the fold-specific estimating functions. All nuisance fitting, nuisance tuning, and aggregation-weight estimation are carried out inside the outer training sample $\mathcal{T}_{k_1}=\mathcal{D}\setminus\mathcal{D}_{k_1}$. Within $\mathcal{T}_{k_1}$, each $k_2\neq k_1$ acts as a secondary calibration fold, and the corresponding initial plug-ins are trained on $\mathcal{D}\setminus(\mathcal{D}_{k_1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{k_2})$.

\paragraph{Round 1: source initialization.}
For each source $s_j$ and secondary fold $k_2\neq k_1$, source $s_j$ fits an initial treated-outcome model on
$\mathcal{D}_{s_j}\setminus(\mathcal{D}_{s_j,k_1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{s_j,k_2})$
using the standard penalized GLM initialization loss in \cref{eq:nuisance_initial_losses}. Denote the result by
$\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{init},s_j,(k_1,k_2)}^{1}$.
The source sends the $K_f-1$ fitted parameter vectors
$\{\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{init},s_j,(k_1,k_2)}^{1}:k_2\neq k_1\}$
to the target. No source records are transmitted, and the initial site/treatment tilting model is not fit at this stage because it requires target-side summaries.

\paragraph{Target summaries.}
For each received initial outcome fit, the target computes two summaries. The first is the calibration-fold derivative summary
\[
\widehat{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t,s_j,(k_1,k_2)}
=
\tildeE_{t,k_2}\!\left[
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
g\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X});\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{init},s_j,(k_1,k_2)}^{1}\}
\right],
\]
which enters the calibrated site/treatment loss. The second is the training-fold feature mean
\[
\widehat{\boldsymbol{b}}_{t,(k_1,k_2)}
=
\tildeE_{t,-(k_1,k_2)}\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})\},
\]
which enters the initial site/treatment loss. The target sends the corresponding pairs
$\{(\widehat{\boldsymbol{h}}_{t,s_j,(k_1,k_2)},\widehat{\boldsymbol{b}}_{t,(k_1,k_2)}):k_2\neq k_1\}$
back to source $s_j$.

\paragraph{Round 2: source calibration.}
After receiving the target summaries, source $s_j$ first computes, for each secondary fold, an initial site/treatment parameter
$\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{init},s_j,(k_1,k_2)}$
by minimizing the fold-specific site/treatment initialization loss in \cref{eq:nuisance_initial_losses} on
$\mathcal{D}_{s_j}\setminus(\mathcal{D}_{s_j,k_1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{s_j,k_2})$,
with $\widehat{\boldsymbol{b}}_{t,(k_1,k_2)}$ used as the target feature mean. It then solves one fold-summed calibrated problem over the secondary folds:
\[
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1}^{(-k_1)}
=
\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
\frac{1}{K_f-1}\sum_{k_2\neq k_1}
\ell_{\gamma,\mathrm{cal}}^{(k_2)}
\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma};
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\mathrm{init},s_j,(k_1,k_2)}^{1}
\right),
\]
and
\[
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,(-k_1)}
=
\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
\frac{1}{K_f-1}\sum_{k_2\neq k_1}
\ell_{\alpha,\mathrm{cal}}^{(k_2)}
\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha};
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{init},s_j,(k_1,k_2)}
\right).
\]
The secondary folds therefore contribute losses to a single calibrated nuisance update, rather than producing separate nuisance estimates that are averaged after fitting.

\paragraph{Source evaluation summaries.}
Using the cross-fitted nuisance pair
$(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,(-k_1)},\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1}^{(-k_1)})$,
source $s_j$ evaluates its outer fold $\mathcal{D}_{s_j,k_1}$ and forms
\[
\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j,k_1}^{1}
=
\tildeE_{s_j,k_1}\!\left[
w(\boldsymbol{X};\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1}^{(-k_1)})
\{Y-g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X});\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,(-k_1)})\}
\right],
\]
together with the corresponding source-side variance component computed from the centered weighted residuals on $\mathcal{D}_{s_j,k_1}$. The source sends
$\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,(-k_1)}$,
$\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j,k_1}^{1}$,
the source variance component, and the relevant source fold size to the target. The tilting parameter can remain local because the target only needs the correction and variance summaries from the source.

\paragraph{TATE aggregation inside the outer training sample.}
The displayed source calibration is performed for both $a=1$ and $a=0$.
For each arm, the target forms the fold-specific source-assisted estimate
\[
\widehat{\mu}_{t,s_j,k_1}^{a}
=
\tildeE_{t,k_1}\!\left[
g\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X});
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{a,(-k_1)}\}
\right]
+\widehat{\delta}_{t,s_j,k_1}^{a},
\]
and then contrasts the arms:
$\widehat\tau_{t,s_j,k_1}
=\widehat\mu_{t,s_j,k_1}^1-\widehat\mu_{t,s_j,k_1}^0$ and
$\widehat\tau_{ot,k_1}
=\widehat\mu_{ot,k_1}^1-\widehat\mu_{ot,k_1}^0$.
The aggregation weights are estimated without using $\mathcal{D}_{k_1}$. Specifically, the target recomputes the target-only component, the target-side source components, and their covariance terms inside $\mathcal{T}_{k_1}$ using the same secondary-fold cross-fitting scheme. Together with the source variance summaries, these quantities enter \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective}. The default implementation fixes the Wald threshold $1/\lambda$ in advance as described in \cref{rem:finite_sample_wald_cutoff}, and solves for $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(-k_1)}$ inside $\mathcal{T}_{k_1}$. If one instead tunes $\lambda$ over a grid, the secondary folds provide the validation summaries: inner-training folds produce penalized candidate weights and the held-out secondary fold evaluates their pure variance criterion, without reapplying the training penalty. No individual-level validation records are shared.

\paragraph{Final fold estimate and variance.}
The one common TATE weight vector
$\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{(-k_1)}$ is applied only to the held-out outer
fold, yielding
\[
\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{agg},k_1}
=\widehat\tau_{ot,k_1}
+\sum_{j=1}^K\widehat\eta_j^{(-k_1)}
(\widehat\tau_{t,s_j,k_1}-\widehat\tau_{ot,k_1}).
\]
Repeating the construction over $k_1=1,\ldots,K_f$ and pooling the
observation-level pseudo-values gives $\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{agg}}$.
The point estimate and variance are computed from the same uncentered influence-function values. For an observation $i$ in evaluation fold $k(i)$, define
\[
\widehat{\Phi}_i^a \coloneqq
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle\frac{N_{\mathrm{all}}}{N_t}\biggl[(1-\textstyle\sum_j\widehat{\eta}_j^{(-k(i))})\widetilde{Y}_{ot,i}
+\textstyle\sum_j\widehat{\eta}_j^{(-k(i))}
g\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X}_i);
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{a,(-k(i))}\}\biggr],
& i\in\mathcal{D}_t,\\[8pt]
\displaystyle\frac{N_{\mathrm{all}}}{N_{s_j}}\,
\widehat{\eta}_j^{(-k(i))}
w(\boldsymbol{X}_i;\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,a}^{(-k(i))})
\{Y_i-g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}_i);
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{a,(-k(i))})\},
& i\in\mathcal{D}_{s_j}.
\end{cases}
\]
Here $\widetilde{Y}_{ot,i}$ denotes the arm-$a$ uncentered target-only AIPW
pseudo-outcome. The TATE pseudo-value is formed \emph{before} computing a
variance,
$\widehat\Phi_i^\tau=\widehat\Phi_i^1-\widehat\Phi_i^0$.
Under independent site-stratified sampling,
\[
\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{agg}}
=N_{\mathrm{all}}^{-1}\sum_i\widehat\Phi_i^\tau,
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}(\widehat\tau_{\mathrm{agg}})
=
\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{all}}^2}
\sum_{r\in\{t,s_1,\ldots,s_K\}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_r}
\bigl(\widehat{\Phi}_i^\tau-\bar{\Phi}_r^\tau\bigr)^2,
\qquad
\bar{\Phi}_r^\tau
=\frac{1}{N_r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{D}_r}\widehat{\Phi}_i^\tau.
\]
Thus the treatment--control covariance is included automatically, and the
same fold-specific weight vector enters both arm components.

\subsection{Linear Outcome One-Round Specialization with Two-Level Cross-Fitting}\label{supp:linear_one_round_cf}

When the outcome model is linear, $g\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X});\boldsymbol{\alpha}\}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^T\phi(\boldsymbol{X})$, the communication-saving version simplifies because
\[
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}g\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X});\boldsymbol{\alpha}\}=\phi(\boldsymbol{X})
\]
does not depend on an initial outcome parameter. Thus the target does not need to send fold-specific initial outcome models to the sources. It only sends fold-specific covariate means needed for calibration, while the source sites compute the linear calibration and weighted least-squares updates locally. The outer fold $k_1$ and secondary fold $k_2$ are still kept separate, so this is the cross-fitted linear specialization of the one-round estimator, not the non-cross-fitted sufficient-statistics shortcut.

For a fixed target-source pair and the treated arm, define the secondary-fold linear calibration loss
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma_loss_linear_cf}
\ell_{\gamma,\mathrm{lin}}^{(k_1,k_2)}(\boldsymbol{\gamma})
=
\tildeE_{t,k_2}\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})\}^T\boldsymbol{\gamma}
+
\tildeE_{s_j,k_2}\!\left[I(A=1)\exp\{-\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T\boldsymbol{\gamma}\}\right]
+
\lambda_{\gamma}\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}\|_1 .
\end{equation}
This is exactly the GLM calibrated $\gamma$-loss with $g'(\cdot)=1$. For the outcome update, each secondary fold uses an initial tilting fit $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{init},(k_1,k_2)}$ trained on $\mathcal{D}_{s_j}\setminus(\mathcal{D}_{s_j,k_1}\cup\mathcal{D}_{s_j,k_2})$ with the corresponding target training-fold mean $\tildeE_{t,-(k_1,k_2)}\{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})\}$. The fold-specific weighted least-squares loss is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:alpha_loss_linear_cf}
\ell_{\alpha,\mathrm{lin}}^{(k_1,k_2)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
=
\tildeE_{s_j,k_2}\!\left[
I(A=1)\exp\{-\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{\mathrm{init},(k_1,k_2)}\}
\{Y-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^T\phi(\boldsymbol{X})\}^2
\right]
+
\lambda_{\alpha}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}\|_1 .
\end{equation}
The nuisance estimators used on the held-out outer fold are obtained by minimizing the fold-summed versions of these two losses:
\[
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1}^{(-k_1)}
\in
\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}
\frac{1}{K_f-1}\sum_{k_2\neq k_1}
\ell_{\gamma,\mathrm{lin}}^{(k_1,k_2)}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}),
\qquad
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,(-k_1)}
\in
\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
\frac{1}{K_f-1}\sum_{k_2\neq k_1}
\ell_{\alpha,\mathrm{lin}}^{(k_1,k_2)}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}).
\]

\section{One-Round Communication Algorithm for Linear Outcome Models}
When the outcome models are linear, i.e., $g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \boldsymbol{\alpha}^T \phi(\boldsymbol{X})$, the framework simplifies significantly. Specifically, the gradient of the outcome model, $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} g(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}); \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \phi(\boldsymbol{X})$, does not depend on the parameter $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$. This breaks the iterative dependency between the outcome and site/treatment models, allowing for a highly efficient one-round estimation at the source sites. The strategy is for the target site to send sufficient statistics that allow source sites to locally perform a sequential estimation.

\subsection{Derivations for One-Round Linear Algorithm}

\subsubsection{Variance and Covariance}

Here we first derive the key formulas that allow variance and covariance terms involving target site data to be computed efficiently using pre-calculated sufficient statistics in the linear model case. For notational simplicity, we write $\boldsymbol{X}$ in place of $\phi(\boldsymbol{X})$ throughout this section.

The target-side variance for the estimator from source $s_j$ is the variance of its influence function component on the target data. By definition, and noting that the mean of the influence function component is zero:
\begin{equation*}
\widehat{V}_{t,s_j} = \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_t}(\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j,i})^2
\end{equation*}
For a linear model, $g(\boldsymbol{X}_i; \boldsymbol{\alpha}) = \boldsymbol{\alpha}^T \boldsymbol{X}_i$. The influence function component is:
\begin{equation*}
\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j,i} = g(\boldsymbol{X}_i; \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^1) - \tildeE_t[g(\boldsymbol{X}; \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^1)] = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T}\boldsymbol{X}_i - \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T}\bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T}(\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)
\end{equation*}
Squaring this term gives a scalar, which can be expressed as a quadratic form:
\begin{equation*}
(\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j,i})^2 = \left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T}(\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)\right) \left((\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)^T \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1}\right) = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T}(\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)(\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)^T \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1}
\end{equation*}
Summing over all $i \in \mathcal{D}_t$ and dividing by $N_t$, the central term becomes the sample covariance matrix of the covariates at the target site, $\widehat{\Sigma}_t$:
\begin{equation*}
\widehat{V}_{t,s_j} = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T} \left( \frac{1}{N_t} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_t} (\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)(\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)^T \right) \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1} = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T} \widehat{\Sigma}_t \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1}
\end{equation*}

This term is the covariance between the target-only influence function and the target-side component from source $s_j$.
\begin{align*}
\widehat{C}_{ot,s_j} &= \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_t}(\widehat{\varphi}_{ot,i} - \bar{\widehat{\varphi}}_{ot})(\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j,i}) \\
&= \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_t}(\widehat{\varphi}_{ot,i} - \bar{\widehat{\varphi}}_{ot})\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T}(\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)\right)
\end{align*}
Since $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T}$ is constant with respect to the sum, we can factor it out:
\begin{align*}
\widehat{C}_{ot,s_j} &= \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T} \left( \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_t}(\widehat{\varphi}_{ot,i} - \bar{\widehat{\varphi}}_{ot})(\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t) \right) \\
&= \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T} \widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{ot,X}
\end{align*}
where $\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{ot,X}$ is the pre-computed vector of covariances between the target-only IF and the covariates.

This term is needed by the target site for the final aggregation. It requires the parameter vectors from two different source sites, $s_j$ and $s_k$.
\begin{align*}
\widehat{C}_{t,s_j,s_k} &= \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_t} (\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_j,i})(\widehat{\zeta}_{t,s_k,i}) \\
&= \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_t} \left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T}(\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)\right) \left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_k}^{1,T}(\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)\right)
\end{align*}
Rewriting the second scalar term as its transpose allows us to form the quadratic expression:
\begin{align*}
\widehat{C}_{t,s_j,s_k} &= \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_t} \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T} (\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t) (\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)^T \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_k}^{1} \\
&= \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T} \left( \frac{1}{N_t}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{D}_t} (\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t) (\boldsymbol{X}_i - \bar{\boldsymbol{X}}_t)^T \right) \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_k}^{1} = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T} \widehat{\Sigma}_t \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_k}^{1}
\end{align*}
This shows that once the target has $\widehat{\Sigma}_t$ and receives the parameter vectors $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1}$ and $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_k}^{1}$, it can compute this final covariance term.

\subsubsection{Loss Functions}
For the linear case, the general loss functions from the main text specialize to the following forms. This allows for a sequential estimation procedure: first $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ is estimated to balance the working features $\phi(\boldsymbol{X})$, and then $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ is estimated using weights derived from $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$.

The loss for $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ becomes a direct covariate balancing objective using an exponential tilting formulation. As it only depends on the features $\phi(\boldsymbol{X})$, it can be minimized first without knowledge of $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:gamma_loss_linear_corrected}
\ell(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}) = \left( \tildeE_t \left[ \phi(\boldsymbol{X}) \right] \right)^T \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1} + \tildeE_{s_j} \left[ I(A=1) e^{-\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}} \right] + \lambda_{\gamma} \|\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{s_j,1}\|_1
\end{equation}
The term $\tildeE_t(\phi(\boldsymbol{X}))$ is the mean covariate vector $\bar{\phi}_t$ sent from the target site.

The outcome model parameters are estimated using weighted least squares (Lasso Regression), which is the specific form of the general GLM loss for linear models. The weights are derived from the calibration/tilting model estimated in the previous step.
\begin{equation} \label{eq:alpha_loss_linear_corrected}
\ell(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^1) = \tildeE_{s_j}\left[ \frac{I(A=1)}{e^{\phi(\boldsymbol{X})^T \widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1}}} (Y - \boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^{1,T}\phi(\boldsymbol{X}))^2 \right] + \lambda_{\alpha} \|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^1\|_1
\end{equation}

\subsection{Algorithm Steps}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE \textbf{Round 1: Single Communication Round}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Target site $t$ computes and sends:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Target-only estimates: $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{ot}^1$, $\widehat{\mu}^1_{ot}$, and $\widehat{V}_{ot}$.
            \item Sufficient statistics from $\mathcal{D}_t$: the covariate mean $\bar{\phi}_t$, the covariate covariance matrix $\widehat{\Sigma}_t$, the sample size $N_t$, and the covariance vector $\widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{ot,X}$.
            \item The target site broadcasts this package of information to all source sites.
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Source sites $s_j$ receive summaries, compute locally, and return:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Each source site first computes its density ratio parameters $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1}$ by minimizing \cref{eq:gamma_loss_linear_corrected} using the received $\bar{\phi}_t$.
            \item Then, it computes its outcome model parameters $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1}$ by minimizing the weighted least squares loss in \cref{eq:alpha_loss_linear_corrected}, using weights derived from the newly computed $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\gamma}}_{s_j,1}$.
            \item Using the received statistics, it computes the final estimator $\widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j}$, the target-side variance $\widehat{V}_{t,s_j} = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T} \widehat{\Sigma}_t \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^1$, and the covariance $\widehat{C}_{ot,s_j} = \widehat{\boldsymbol{C}}_{ot,X}^T \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^1$.
            \item Using its own data, it computes the source-side variance $\widehat{V}_{s_j}$.
            \item Each source site $s_j$ sends back to the target: $\widehat{\mu}^1_{t,s_j}$, $\widehat{V}_{t,s_j}$, $\widehat{V}_{s_j}$, $\widehat{C}_{ot,s_j}$, $N_{s_j}$, and the parameter vector $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1}$ (needed for cross-site covariances).
        \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Target site $t$ receives summaries and performs final aggregation:}
        \begin{itemize}
            \item For each pair of source sites $(s_j, s_k)$, the target computes the cross-site covariance: $\widehat{C}_{t,s_j,s_k} = \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_j}^{1,T} \widehat{\Sigma}_t \widehat{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t,s_k}^{1}$.
            \item Repeat these computations for $a=0$, form the treated-minus-control influence summaries, and solve \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective} once to obtain the common TATE weights $\widehat{\boldsymbol{\eta}}$ and final estimate $\widehat{\tau}_{\mathrm{agg}}$.
        \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\end{algorithmic}
