\begin{abstract}
Integrating evidence across healthcare institutions can improve treatment-effect estimation for patient populations with limited local data. Such integration must address confounding, differences between populations, and restrictions on sharing individual records. Relevant adjustment variables may be hidden among many candidate measurements, and regularized model fitting can introduce bias into subsequent causal inference. We propose robust federated causal estimation (RoCE) for a prespecified target population. RoCE uses outcomes from each source treatment group to augment outcome predictions in the target population. A single joint-weight model adjusts treatment assignment and population shift, allowing paired regularized calibration of the outcome and weighting models. Pivotal selection then penalizes incompatible source treatment groups while jointly optimizing borrowing for the target treatment contrast. With target-based initialization, the procedure uses one batched round of model and summary exchange. Under suitable identification, sparsity, and source-separation conditions, RoCE provides model-doubly-robust inference and has asymptotic variance no greater than calibrated target-only estimation. Simulations demonstrate precision gains with compatible sources and protection against substantial source bias, while revealing undercoverage for small departures from compatibility. An All of Us observational drug comparison with 181 clinical features and a supplementary right heart catheterization analysis illustrate applications to prespecified target populations.
\end{abstract}

\noindent{\it Keywords:} causal inference, calibration, federated learning, high-dimensional data, transportability

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Electronic health records (EHRs) collected during routine clinical care provide real-world data for studying treatment effectiveness and safety in the populations receiving those treatments \citep{sherman2016real,hernan2016using}. Longitudinal records of diagnoses, prescriptions, and clinical encounters make it possible to examine treatment use and outcomes beyond the settings of individual clinical trials. Research networks extend this opportunity by connecting institutions whose local samples may be insufficient for a treatment comparison, particularly when eligible patients or outcome events are uncommon. The scientific question, however, often concerns a particular patient population, such as the patients served by one healthcare institution. Additional institutions then provide potential sources of information for that target. When individual records must remain local, this collaboration requires methods that improve target-specific estimation through the exchange of model parameters and summary statistics.

Confounding adjustment is central to such observational comparisons because treatment choices depend on patients' clinical histories. EHRs offer a broad set of candidate adjustment variables, but the measurements that capture these histories may be dispersed among many diagnosis codes, medication records, and utilization features. A small set of prespecified variables may therefore omit information relevant to treatment selection or prognosis. For example, \citet{hou2021comparison} combined a multiple sclerosis registry with linked EHRs and used data-driven feature selection to identify additional adjustment variables absent from the registry. Regularization makes analysis of these large feature sets feasible, yet the resulting selection and shrinkage errors can enter a treatment-effect estimate at first order \citep{belloni2014inference,chernozhukov2018double}. Valid inference must account for these errors, particularly when the local samples supporting nuisance estimation are much smaller than the full network sample.

A natural starting point for multisite analysis is meta-analysis of local treatment-effect estimates, using sample-size or inverse-variance weights. Each site can use augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW), which combines an outcome regression with a treatment propensity model and is consistent under the identification conditions if either model is correct \citep{robins1994estimation,bang2005doubly}. Federated causal methods extend information sharing through Bayesian synthesis and distributed estimation using locally computed quantities \citep{vo2022bayesian,xiong2023federated}. Such approaches enable collaboration without pooling patient records. The population defining the causal effect nevertheless remains important: when treatment effects vary with patient characteristics, averaging effects from different sites need not recover the target average treatment effect (TATE). Estimating the TATE therefore requires accounting for how source populations differ from the target.

Target-specific borrowing addresses this requirement by adjusting source observations to the target population. Calibration weighting aligns source and target covariate distributions \citep{josey2022calibration,chattopadhyay2024one}, while multiply robust federated estimation accommodates covariate shift and differences in the covariates available across sites \citep{Han2023multiply}. Adjustment for covariate shift does not, by itself, ensure that the conditional outcome relationship is shared across populations \citep{cole2010generalizing,pearl2014external,dahabreh2019generalizing}. Federated adaptive causal estimation (FACE) addresses this additional concern by penalizing source contributions that disagree with target-only estimation; its FACE-X extension uses flexible nuisance estimators with consistency and product-rate conditions \citep{han2025federated}. This adaptive borrowing perspective is especially relevant when compatibility is unknown and may differ between treatment arms.

High-dimensional distributed causal estimation addresses a complementary part of the problem. Communication-efficient covariate-balancing methods fit high-dimensional propensity and outcome models using distributed summaries \citep{wang2024communication}. DisC2o-HD incorporates cross-site covariate shift while approximating the estimator available from pooled data \citep{Tong2025disc2o}. These developments show how high-dimensional adjustment can be coordinated across institutions. Here we seek to combine this ability to adjust for many covariates with estimation for a prespecified target and adaptation to incompatible source outcomes. The nuisance-fitting step must also support inference when one working model is misspecified. This objective calls for linking adaptive source aggregation to calibration of the target estimating equation.

The single-site literature provides several tools for controlling errors from high-dimensional nuisance estimation, including double selection, approximate residual balancing, and double machine learning \citep{belloni2014inference,athey2018approximate,chernozhukov2018double}. These approaches combine variable selection or flexible fitting with estimating procedures that reduce the impact of first-stage error under their respective assumptions. For doubly robust estimators, an additional distinction is consequential: consistency when either model is correct does not automatically imply valid confidence intervals under the same condition. If the other model is misspecified, regularization error in the correctly specified model can contribute at first order. Regularized calibration addresses this problem by choosing fitting losses according to the sensitivity of the effect estimator \citep{vermeulen2015bias,tan2020model,smucler2019unifying}.

For AIPW, calibration exploits the pairing between the outcome regression and propensity model: differentiating the estimating equation with respect to one model motivates how the other should be fitted. \citet{tan2020model} develops penalized calibration with model-assisted confidence intervals that remain valid under a correct propensity model even when the outcome model is misspecified; with a linear outcome model, the intervals are doubly robust. \citet{smucler2019unifying} combines derivative-matched losses, sample splitting, and cross-fitting to obtain rate and model double robustness for a broader class of causal contrasts. \citet{hou2025efficient} uses a two-layer cross-fitting construction for sequential calibrated fitting in semi-supervised treatment-effect estimation with high-dimensional logistic models. Together, these results motivate fitting nuisance models to support inference for the final causal estimand, with the data separation tailored to the sequence of fitting steps.

Extending this paired calibration to transported estimation introduces a third nuisance function: a source-to-target density ratio, in addition to the outcome regression and source treatment propensity. Calibrating the two weighting components separately does not automatically calibrate their product in the transported score. We propose robust federated causal estimation (RoCE) by returning to the role of source observations: outcomes in a source treatment group augment outcome predictions for the entire target population. A single joint weight maps that treatment group to the target, combining treatment and population adjustment. This construction restores an outcome--weight pair and yields paired regularized calibration losses for the source-assisted target mean. Under suitable identification, sparsity, and regularity conditions, calibration supports inference when either of these two working models is correct.

Calibration controls nuisance-estimation error within a source-assisted estimate, while aggregation must address bias from incompatible source outcomes. Building on FACE, RoCE compares each source-assisted estimate with a calibrated target-only estimate on a common standard-error scale and uses a pivotal penalty to limit discrepant contributions. A source can contribute to one treatment arm and receive zero weight in the other. The two arms' weights are optimized jointly for the variance of their difference, accounting for dependence induced by shared target observations. Two-layer cross-fitting accommodates the sequential calibration steps and reserves observations for final evaluation after nuisance fitting and weight learning. With target-based outcome initialization, the procedure requires one batched target broadcast and one source return containing coefficients and empirical summaries. RoCE thus connects calibrated high-dimensional fitting, adaptive borrowing, and federated implementation around a common target treatment contrast.

Our main contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),leftmargin=*,itemsep=0pt]
\item Joint weighting and paired calibration enable target-specific borrowing with high-dimensional covariates and model-doubly-robust inference under the stated conditions.
\item Arm-specific pivotal aggregation limits incompatible borrowing and attains an oracle variance no greater than calibrated target-only estimation under the stated source-selection conditions.
\item A federated implementation combines calibrated fitting and joint treatment-effect aggregation in one batched round of communication without transferring individual records.
\end{enumerate}

The Supplementary Material provides implementation details, proofs, alternative constructions, and a right heart catheterization application.

\section{Setting and Identification}\label{sec:notation}

Consider one prespecified target site $t$ and $K$ sources $\mathcal S=\{s_1,\ldots,s_K\}$. Individual $i$ has observed record $D_i=(R_i,\boldsymbol X_i,A_i,Y_i)$, where $R_i\in\{t\}\cup\mathcal S$ denotes site membership, $\boldsymbol X_i\in\mathbb R^p$ baseline covariates, $A_i\in\{0,1\}$ treatment, and $Y_i$ the outcome. Let $\mathcal D_r=\{i:R_i=r\}$ index the observations at site $r$, with $n_r=|\mathcal D_r|$ and $N=n_t+\sum_jn_{s_j}$. Write $\E_r$ and $P_r$ for expectation and probability conditional on $R_i=r$. The sitewise empirical average of $h$ is $\En_r h=n_r^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal D_r}h(D_i)$; $I(\cdot)$ denotes an indicator.

Let $Y_i(a)$ be the potential outcome under treatment $a$. Our estimands are the target potential-outcome means and their contrast,
\[
\mu_t^a=\E_t\{Y_i(a)\},\qquad a\in\{0,1\},
\qquad \tau_t=\mu_t^1-\mu_t^0.
\]
The target population is fixed before borrowing. Source observations add information without redefining the TATE. For binary outcomes, $\tau_t$ is the marginal risk difference in the target population.

We distinguish target identification from source eligibility. Let $e_r^{a,\star}(\boldsymbol x)=P_r(A_i=a\mid\boldsymbol X_i=\boldsymbol x)$ be the true treatment probability and $f_r^\star$ the covariate density with respect to a common dominating measure. Stars mark true functions; working models follow in Method.

\begin{assumption}[Target identification and source eligibility]\label{asmp:identification}
For each $a\in\{0,1\}$, let $\mathcal S_a^*\subseteq\mathcal S$ be the possibly empty transportable source set. For constants $\epsilon>0$ and $C<\infty$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),ref=\ref{asmp:identification}(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item\label{asmp:consistency} \emph{Consistency and no interference.} At the target and eligible sources, treatment is well defined with no interference, and $Y_i=Y_i(A_i)$.
\item\label{asmp:exchangeability} \emph{Target identification.} $Y_i(a)\perp A_i\mid\boldsymbol X_i,R_i=t$ and $e_t^{a,\star}(\boldsymbol X_i)\in[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]$.
\item\label{asmp:positivity} \emph{Source exchangeability and overlap.} For each $s_j\in\mathcal S_a^*$, $Y_i(a)\perp A_i\mid\boldsymbol X_i,R_i=s_j$ and $e_{s_j}^{a,\star}(\boldsymbol X_i)\in[\epsilon,1-\epsilon]$. In addition,
\[
\operatorname{supp}(f_t^\star)=\operatorname{supp}(f_{s_j}^\star),\qquad
\|f_t^\star/f_{s_j}^\star\|_\infty\le C.
\]
\item\label{asmp:transportability} \emph{Conditional mean transportability.} For each $s_j\in\mathcal S_a^*$, $\E_{s_j}\{Y_i(a)\mid\boldsymbol X_i\}=\E_t\{Y_i(a)\mid\boldsymbol X_i\}$ on the common support.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

Parts (a) and (b) link observed outcomes to potential outcomes and require the measured covariates to account for target-site confounding, with treatment overlap. They identify
\[
\mu_t^a=\E_t\!\left\{\E_t(Y_i\mid A_i=a,\boldsymbol X_i)\right\}.
\]
Thus target data identify the TATE even when both source sets are empty. Borrowing aims to improve precision under additional source conditions.

Parts (c) and (d) provide those conditions: adjustment for source-site confounding, population overlap, and a shared conditional potential-outcome mean \citep{cole2010generalizing,pearl2014external,dahabreh2019generalizing}. For an eligible source, they also give $\mu_t^a=\E_t\{\E_{s_j}(Y_i\mid A_i=a,\boldsymbol X_i)\}$. The source conditional mean is therefore averaged over the target covariate distribution. Covariate distributions, treatment policies, and residual variation may differ across sites. Eligibility is arm-specific: a source may contribute to one potential-outcome mean without contributing to the other. Equality of conditional treatment effects alone does not justify transporting the two means separately.

Source eligibility is unknown, and measured balance cannot establish either unconfoundedness or outcome compatibility. All sources remain candidates, with discrepancies from target-only estimation guiding adaptive borrowing \citep{han2025federated}. The theory distinguishes transportable sources from candidates whose population limits equal the target arm mean. We call the latter \emph{unbiased candidates}, without asserting finite-sample unbiasedness.

Following the stage notation of \citet{hou2025efficient}, hats denote estimates and bars population coefficients; the subscript $\mathrm{init}$ identifies initial fits and their population minimizers. Calibrated coefficients omit this subscript, and stars identify true nuisance functions and their coefficients when correctly specified. A \emph{joint weight} $q_{s_j}^a$ adjusts observations from a source treatment group; an \emph{aggregation weight} $\eta_{s_j}^a$ combines the resulting candidate with other estimates. The next section constructs calibrated source-assisted estimates and aggregates them for $\tau_t$.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}

RoCE implements the two tasks described in the Introduction: calibrated estimation of each source-assisted target mean, followed by adaptive borrowing across source treatment groups. We begin with one source and one treatment arm, combining target outcome predictions with a weighted source residual correction. The resulting population mean determines the calibration losses and the fitted candidate estimator. We then combine these candidates with target-only estimation, choosing both arms' weights for the treatment contrast. \Cref{sec:algorithms} completes the construction with cross-fitting and federated communication; fitting and weight learning exclude the observations used for final evaluation.

\subsection{Working Models and Source-Assisted Target Means}\label{sec:nuisance}

Fix a source $s_j$ and treatment arm $a$. Let $m_r^{a,\star}(\boldsymbol x)=\E_r(Y_i\mid A_i=a,\boldsymbol X_i=\boldsymbol x)$ denote the true outcome regression at site $r$. For a transportable source, this conditional mean agrees with $m_t^{a,\star}(\boldsymbol X)$, and averaging it over target covariates identifies $\mu_t^a$. We approximate the mean using $m(\boldsymbol x;\alpha)=g\{\phi(\boldsymbol x)^T\alpha\}$, where $\alpha\in\mathbb R^d$ and $g$ is a canonical generalized linear model (GLM) inverse link. Examples include logistic, linear, and Poisson means; correct specification concerns the conditional mean without requiring the full exponential-family distribution. All sites use the same prespecified feature map $\phi$, including an intercept. Its dimension $d$ may differ from the raw covariate dimension $p$; with main effects only, $\phi(\boldsymbol X_i)=(1,\boldsymbol X_i^T)^T$ and $d=p+1$.

The working outcome model may be misspecified. Observed outcomes from source arm $a$ can correct its target predictions if the source residuals are appropriately weighted. Because this correction uses only one source treatment group, its ideal weight is
\[
q_{s_j}^{a,\star}(\boldsymbol x)=\frac{f_t^\star(\boldsymbol x)}{f_{s_j}^\star(\boldsymbol x)e_{s_j}^{a,\star}(\boldsymbol x)}.
\]
The denominator equals $P_{s_j}(A_i=a)f_{s_j}^\star(\boldsymbol x\mid A_i=a)$, so the weight maps the source treatment group to the entire target covariate population. The arm-probability factor accounts for averaging over all source observations. In particular, the ideal weight satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:balancing_property}
\E_{s_j}\{I(A=a)q_{s_j}^{a,\star}(\boldsymbol X)h(\boldsymbol X)\}
=\E_t\{h(\boldsymbol X)\}
\end{equation}
for integrable $h$. Taking $h=1$ gives the normalization $\E_{s_j}\{I(A=a)q_{s_j}^{a,\star}(\boldsymbol X)\}=1$.

RoCE models this joint adjustment directly through $q_{s_j}^a(\boldsymbol x;\gamma)=\exp\{-\phi(\boldsymbol x)^T\gamma\}$, where $\gamma\in\mathbb R^d$ and the intercept accommodates the arm normalization. This formulation combines treatment and population adjustment in one weighting model, paired with $m(\boldsymbol X;\alpha)$. A correct weighting model represents the ideal function $q_{s_j}^{a,\star}(\boldsymbol X)$; balancing a finite set of features alone does not establish that property. The two working models define the source-assisted population mean
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_source_functional}
\mu_{s_j}^a(\alpha,\gamma)
=\E_t\{m(\boldsymbol X;\alpha)\}
+\E_{s_j}\!\left[I(A=a)q_{s_j}^a(\boldsymbol X;\gamma)
\{Y-m(\boldsymbol X;\alpha)\}\right].
\end{equation}
The first term averages outcome predictions over the target population, and the second corrects those predictions using weighted source residuals. Thus the subscript $s_j$ identifies the source supplying the correction, while the estimand remains $\mu_t^a$. The coefficient-dependent quantity $\mu_{s_j}^a(\alpha,\gamma)$ equals that estimand under the conditions below. This transported AIPW construction builds on \citet{josey2022calibration,han2025federated}.

\subsection{Regularized Calibration}\label{sec:calibration}

When one working model is misspecified, estimation error in the correctly specified model can affect $\mu_{s_j}^a(\alpha,\gamma)$ at first order. Calibration chooses fitting losses to remove this sensitivity at the population limits \citep{tan2020model,smucler2019unifying}. Continue with the fixed source and arm, and write $\phi=\phi(\boldsymbol X)$ in the derivatives below. The relevant derivatives are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_calibration_derivatives}
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_\alpha\mu_{s_j}^a(\alpha,\gamma)
&=\E_t\{g'(\phi^T\alpha)\phi\}
-\E_{s_j}\{I(A=a)q_{s_j}^a(\boldsymbol X;\gamma) g'(\phi^T\alpha)\phi\},\\
\nabla_\gamma\mu_{s_j}^a(\alpha,\gamma)
&=-\E_{s_j}\{I(A=a)q_{s_j}^a(\boldsymbol X;\gamma)(Y-m(\boldsymbol X;\alpha))\phi\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The first derivative motivates fitting the joint weight to balance derivative-weighted features between target and source. The second motivates fitting the outcome model to balance weighted source residuals. These are the two conditions that the paired losses seek to satisfy at their population limits. If both derivatives vanish and second derivatives are locally bounded, perturbing the fitted coefficients changes the population mean only at second order.

To implement this pairing, let $L(Y,u)=\Psi(u)-Yu$, with $\Psi'=g$, and let $\|v\|_{1,-0}$ denote the sum of absolute slope coefficients, excluding the intercept. We first fit an $\ell_1$-penalized outcome GLM in target arm $a$ and an initial exponential-balancing weight at each source. Denote their coefficients by $\widehat\alpha_{\mathrm{init}}$ and $\widehat\gamma_{\mathrm{init}}$; the target outcome initialization is shared across sources. These initial fits supply fixed multipliers for the subsequent calibrated updates, avoiding alternating optimization of the two models. The initial losses are given in \cref{supp:primary_source_fits}.

For stable multipliers, fix $M>0$ and clip each initial linear predictor using $\mathcal T_M(u)=\operatorname{sign}(u)\min(|u|,2M)$. The corresponding clipped logistic link is $\sigma_M(u)=\operatorname{expit}\{\mathcal T_M(u)\}$. Following the clipping convention of \citet{hou2025efficient}, the theory bounds the relevant population predictors by $M$, leaving a margin before the numerical radius $2M$; choosing a numerical radius alone does not establish this population bound.

With the initial fits held fixed, use the derivative multiplier $d_{\mathrm{init}}(\boldsymbol X)=g'\{\mathcal T_M(\phi^T\widehat\alpha_{\mathrm{init}})\}$ and the weight multiplier $\omega_{\mathrm{init}}(\boldsymbol X)=e^{-\mathcal T_M(\phi^T\widehat\gamma_{\mathrm{init}})}$. The two calibration losses are
\begin{align}
\ell_{\mathrm{RCAL}}(\gamma;\widehat\alpha_{\mathrm{init}})
&=\En_t(d_{\mathrm{init}}\phi)^T\gamma
+\En_{s_j}\{I(A=a)e^{-\phi^T\gamma}d_{\mathrm{init}}\}
+\lambda_\gamma\|\gamma\|_{1,-0},\label{eq:gamma_calibrated_loss}\\
\ell_{\mathrm{RWL}}(\alpha;\widehat\gamma_{\mathrm{init}})
&=\En_{s_j}\left[I(A=a)\omega_{\mathrm{init}}(\boldsymbol X)
L(Y,\phi^T\alpha)\right]
+\lambda_\alpha\|\alpha\|_{1,-0}.\label{eq:alpha_calibrated_loss}
\end{align}

Let $\widehat\gamma$ and $\widehat\alpha$ minimize these losses, respectively. Each optimization is convex in its coefficient vector when $g'\ge0$. The joint-weight loss uses the same derivative multiplier in its target and source terms, preserving balance under a correct weight even when the initial outcome fit is misspecified. The outcome loss fits source residuals under the initial joint weight. Only the target moment $\En_t(d_{\mathrm{init}}\phi)$ is needed at the source, which is the basis for the federated implementation below. The empirical averages here represent calibration data; \cref{sec:algorithms} specifies their folds and the separate data used for initial fitting.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[Calibration under either correct model]\label{lem:main_calibration}
\FACEHDSourceCalibration
\end{lemma}

The correctly specified model supplies one zero derivative; stationarity of the paired calibrated loss supplies the other. Initial and calibrated coefficients may differ for the misspecified model. The \hyperref[proof:main_calibration]{proof of \cref*{lem:main_calibration}} is given in \cref{supp:pairwise_technical_lemmas}; \cref{lem:pairwise_nuisance_rates} then controls error propagation from the initial fits.

For a linear outcome mean, $g'=1$, so the procedure simplifies to exponential feature balancing and penalized weighted least squares; nonlinear means retain the derivative multiplier.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
We now return to the source and arm indices. Denote the calibrated functions by $\widehat m_{s_j}^a(\boldsymbol x)=m(\boldsymbol x;\widehat{\alpha}_{t,s_j}^a)$ and $\widehat q_{s_j}^a=q_{s_j}^a(\cdot;\widehat{\gamma}_{s_j,a})$, without further clipping of these source-assisted fits. Inserting them into the empirical counterpart of \cref{eq:main_source_functional} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:source_assisted_mu1}
\widehat\mu_{s_j}^a
=\En_t\{\widehat m_{s_j}^a(\boldsymbol X)\}
+\En_{s_j}\left[I(A=a)\widehat q_{s_j}^a(\boldsymbol X)
\{Y-\widehat m_{s_j}^a(\boldsymbol X)\}\right].
\end{equation}
This is the fitted candidate corresponding to $\mu_{s_j}^a(\alpha,\gamma)$. All candidates seek the same target mean $\mu_t^a$, although an incompatible source can have a different population limit. The cross-fitting procedure below specifies when this expression is evaluated on training data for weight learning and when its contributions are evaluated on held-out data for final estimation.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
To assess whether borrowing is helpful, we also need a candidate that relies only on the target's own identification conditions. Calibrated target outcome and logistic propensity fits $\widehat m_t^a$ and $\widehat e_t^a$ give
\begin{equation}\label{eq:target_only_mu}
\widehat\mu_t^a=\En_t\left[
\widehat m_t^a(\boldsymbol X)
+\frac{I(A=a)}{\widehat e_t^a(\boldsymbol X)}
\{Y-\widehat m_t^a(\boldsymbol X)\}\right].
\end{equation}
We use regularized calibrated target outcome and logistic propensity fits following \citet{tan2020model,smucler2019unifying,hou2025efficient}; the losses and fold updates are given in \cref{supp:target_only_anchor}. The two calibrated arm-specific propensity fits need not sum to one because each is fitted for its own arm score. The true treatment probabilities still satisfy $e_t^{0,\star}=1-e_t^{1,\star}$.

The target-only candidate supplies the reference for borrowing. Comparing each source-assisted candidate with this reference connects the calibrated estimation step to adaptive aggregation.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\subsection{Joint Aggregation for the Target Treatment Effect}\label{sec:adaptive_aggregation}

Calibration addresses regularization error within a candidate, while aggregation determines how much to borrow from each source treatment group. We balance the precision available from a source against its discrepancy from target-only estimation. All quantities used to learn these weights are computed outside an outer evaluation fold $k_1$. Let $n_{r,-k_1}$ be the remaining sample size at site $r$ and $n_{-k_1}=\sum_r n_{r,-k_1}$. In this subsection, the candidate means and fitted functions use only these training observations, and we suppress their fit index $(-k_1)$.

For arm $a$, let $\eta^a=(\eta_{s_1}^a,\ldots,\eta_{s_K}^a)^T$ collect the source aggregation weights. Starting from the target-only reference, the aggregate adds a weighted discrepancy for each source:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_arm_aggregate}
\widehat\mu_{\mathrm{agg}}^a(\eta^a)
=\widehat\mu_t^a+\sum_{j=1}^K\eta_{s_j}^a
 (\widehat\mu_{s_j}^a-\widehat\mu_t^a).
\end{equation}
The corresponding treatment contrast is $\widehat\tau_t(\eta^1,\eta^0)=\widehat\mu_{\mathrm{agg}}^1(\eta^1)-\widehat\mu_{\mathrm{agg}}^0(\eta^0)$. A source can contribute to one arm and receive zero weight in the other. For example, a source with a shifted treated outcome mean but a compatible control mean may still help estimate $\mu_t^0$. Joint optimization allows this distinction while accounting for covariance between the two estimated means. Imposing $\eta^1=\eta^0$ would instead aggregate assisted treatment effects using a common source-weight vector.

Choosing the two weight vectors jointly requires the variance of their treatment contrast. Stack the weights as $\eta=((\eta^1)^T,(\eta^0)^T)^T$ and write the aggregate in terms of contributions from individual sites. Using \cref{eq:target_only_mu,eq:source_assisted_mu1}, with the arguments $\boldsymbol X_i$ suppressed, these contributions are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_site_contributions}
\begin{aligned}
\widehat G_{t,i}^a(\eta^a)&=\left(1-\sum_j\eta_{s_j}^a\right)
\left[\widehat m_t^a+\frac{I(A_i=a)}{\widehat e_t^a}(Y_i-\widehat m_t^a)\right]
+\sum_j\eta_{s_j}^a\widehat m_{s_j}^a,\\
\widehat G_{s_j,i}^a(\eta^a)&=\eta_{s_j}^a I(A_i=a)\widehat q_{s_j}^a
(Y_i-\widehat m_{s_j}^a).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Averaging within sites and then summing recovers \cref{eq:main_arm_aggregate}. Under the conditions in \cref{sec:theory}, calibration removes first-order nuisance-estimation terms, leaving a leading variance that adds across independent sites. The candidates themselves remain correlated because they share target predictions and both arms use the same site records. Computing the variance of the within-site contrast retains these covariances.

\Needspace{8\baselineskip}
\begin{lemma}[Sitewise variance and cross-arm covariance]\label{lem:main_covariance}
\FACEHDJointCovariance
\end{lemma}

The \hyperref[proof:main_covariance]{proof of \cref*{lem:main_covariance}} is given in \cref{supp:joint_training_moments}. Its covariance-block construction in \cref{supp:aggregation_setup} determines the required summaries: prediction and residual means, variances, cross-arm moments, and site counts. Their transmission is described in \cref{sec:algorithms}. Candidate means are pooled within each site's training sample before discrepancies are computed.

Let $\widehat d_{s_j}^a=\widehat\mu_{s_j}^a-\widehat\mu_t^a$ be the training-sample discrepancy and $\widehat s_{s_j}^a$ its score-based standard error, including shared-target covariance. Set $\widehat t_{s_j}^a=|\widehat d_{s_j}^a|/\widehat s_{s_j}^a$. This standardization compares discrepancies on a common sampling-error scale. We use the term \emph{pivotal selection} for the resulting penalty within the adaptive aggregation framework \citep{han2025federated}. For a positive cutoff $c_n$, choose both weight vectors in one optimization:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:agg_penalized_objective}
\widehat\eta^{(-k_1)}\in\arg\min_{\eta\in\mathbb R^{2K}}
\left\{n_{-k_1}\widehat V_\tau^{(-k_1)}(\eta)
+\sum_{a=0}^1\sum_{j=1}^K(\widehat t_{s_j}^a/c_{n_{-k_1}}-1)_+|\eta_{s_j}^a|\right\}.
\end{equation}
The variance is quadratic in $\eta$, so the objective is a convex quadratic plus a weighted $\ell_1$ penalty. The weights may be signed; the target coefficient for arm $a$ is $1-\sum_j\eta_{s_j}^a$, and $\eta=0$ recovers target-only estimation. Positive-definite curvature ensures a finite, unique optimizer. The penalty is zero below the cutoff and grows above it. Selection occurs within the optimization, without a preceding hard-screening step; exceeding the cutoff need not force a weight to zero in finite samples. Penalties remain arm-specific even if the two source-arm biases cancel in their treatment contrast.

Separate armwise variance minimization generally differs from this joint optimization because the cross-arm covariance depends on both weight vectors. If all retained outcome functions equal their correct transportable limits, the cross-arm covariance of the limiting aggregate means is instead $n_{t,-k_1}^{-1}\operatorname{Cov}_t\{m_t^{1,\star}(\boldsymbol X),m_t^{0,\star}(\boldsymbol X)\}$, independent of the weights. In this special case, the joint and separate-arm oracle minimizers coincide despite nonzero covariance. Finite-sample optimizers need not coincide, and outcome misspecification generally removes this special structure.

\begin{remark}[Distinct roles of the two thresholds]\label{rem:finite_sample_wald_cutoff}
The predictor clipping radius $2M$ belongs to nuisance estimation. The aggregation cutoff $c_n$, with $n=n_{-k_1}$ during weight learning, controls the arm-specific Wald penalties. For theory, $c_n\to\infty$ and $c_n/\sqrt n\to0$, for example $c_n=\sqrt{\log(n\vee3)}$. The implementation parameter multiplying $\widehat t_{s_j}^a$ in the penalty is $\lambda_{\mathrm{agg}}=1/c_n$; a fixed cutoff $2$ corresponds to $\lambda_{\mathrm{agg}}=0.5$. A fixed cutoff is a finite-sample specification, not the growing-cutoff regime of the theorem.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Two-Layer Cross-Fitting and Communication}\label{sec:algorithms}

The preceding construction has three fitting stages: initial nuisance estimation, calibrated updating, and aggregation-weight learning. We adapt the two-level cross-fitting construction of \citet{hou2025efficient} to preserve this order. Initial fits exclude the observations used for their calibration updates, and an outer fold is reserved for evaluation after all three stages. Partition each site $r$ into a fixed number $K_f\ge3$ of balanced folds $\mathcal I_{r,1},\ldots,\mathcal I_{r,K_f}$, shared by both arms, with membership fixed in advance or randomized independently of the observed records. Let $\mathcal I_{r,-k_1,-k_2}=\mathcal D_r\setminus(\mathcal I_{r,k_1}\cup\mathcal I_{r,k_2})$, $\mathcal I_{r,-k_1}=\mathcal D_r\setminus\mathcal I_{r,k_1}$, and $n_{r,J}=|\mathcal I_{r,J}|$. We write $\En_{r,J}h=n_{r,J}^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal I_{r,J}}h(D_i)$ for a fold or fold complement $J$. For an outer fold $k_1$, proceed as follows.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.,leftmargin=*,itemsep=.5\baselineskip]
\item \textbf{Initial fitting and calibration.} For each inner fold $k_2\ne k_1$, fit the initial models outside both $k_1$ and $k_2$. The target outcome initialization is shared across sources. Insert each initial fit into the calibrated loss on fold $k_2$, then pool the loss blocks over $k_2\ne k_1$ and minimize once for each calibrated coefficient vector. Each site's blocks are weighted by its own fold fractions, and each slope penalty enters once. The source updates are in \cref{supp:primary_source_fits}; the corresponding local target updates are in \cref{supp:target_only_anchor}.
\item \textbf{Joint weight learning.} Evaluate the final calibrated fits on all observations outside $k_1$. Pool candidate means and the full two-arm covariance blocks within sites, then compute discrepancies and solve \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective}. This yields one joint pair $\widehat\eta^{(-k_1)}$. The calibration folds contribute fitted training scores here; they are not independent validation data for the final calibrated fits.
\Needspace{4\baselineskip}
\item \textbf{Outer evaluation.} Apply these calibrated fits and the learned weight pair to fold $k_1$. Combine observations using each site's full sample size, retaining the fits and weights specific to that outer fold. All fitting, preprocessing, tuning and weight learning exclude $k_1$.
\end{enumerate}
Thus each initial fit uses $K_f-2$ folds, each calibration update uses loss blocks from the $K_f-1$ folds outside $k_1$, and $k_1$ remains untouched until evaluation. For an observation in outer fold $k_1(i)$, evaluate \cref{eq:main_site_contributions} using nuisance fits and weights trained outside that fold, and write $\widehat G_{r,i}^a=\widehat G_{r,i}^a(\widehat\eta^{a,(-k_1(i))})$ for its held-out contribution. The final estimator, including unequal site and fold sizes, is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_complete_tate}
\widehat\tau_t
=\frac1{n_t}\sum_{i\in\mathcal D_t}(\widehat G_{t,i}^1-\widehat G_{t,i}^0)
 +\sum_j\frac1{n_{s_j}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal D_{s_j}}
                    (\widehat G_{s_j,i}^1-\widehat G_{s_j,i}^0).
\end{equation}
The two data-separation requirements have different roles. Initial fits exclude their calibration fold, and final evaluation excludes all fitting and weight learning. Step 2 reuses observations that fitted the calibrated models; \cref{asmp:main_training_moments} states the training moments needed for weight learning. The primary algorithm and reported analyses use this two-layer construction. An alternative reserves independent inner validation folds for weight learning, with $K_f\ge4$; its algorithm and supporting argument are collected in \cref{supp:two_level_cf,lem:nested_validation}.

The same fitting order admits a federated implementation. With target outcome initialization, all fold-specific initial coefficients, feature means and derivative moments can be sent to sources in one batch. Sources return calibrated outcome coefficients and training/evaluation residual summaries, including both-arm second moments and counts, in one batch. The target computes prediction covariances and joint aggregation weights locally. Every message identifies its training sample and evaluation fold; outer-evaluation summaries never enter weight learning. This one-round protocol is shown in \cref{fig:main_communication}.

Here a communication round comprises one target broadcast and one source return. The number of transmitted coefficients and moments grows with the working dimension, sources, and fold-specific tasks, while the message contents do not contain individual records. The round count assumes that the feature definitions and fitting tasks are agreed in advance and that tuning requests no additional target summaries. Cross-validation is confined to the relevant training sample; \cref{sec:tuning} gives its loss and stabilization conventions. Preprocessing that requires pooled information must be accounted for separately. Source-based outcome initialization requires one additional exchange of target derivative moments (\cref{supp:expanded_two_round}).

\section{Theory}\label{sec:theory}

The analysis follows the two stages of RoCE: calibrated candidate estimation and adaptive borrowing. Candidate expansions control nuisance-estimation error. A separate training-moment argument and source-separation conditions then support weight learning. Together, they yield the source-selection and inference theorem for the final contrast in \cref{eq:main_complete_tate}, followed by variance estimation and an efficiency comparison.

Throughout, $N\to\infty$, the numbers of sources $K$ and folds $K_f$ are fixed, and $d$ may grow. Site fractions satisfy $n_r/N\to\rho_r^{\mathrm{all}}>0$. We study the two-layer procedure in \cref{sec:algorithms}, building on calibrated estimation \citep{tan2020model,smucler2019unifying,hou2025efficient} and adaptive borrowing \citep{han2025federated}. The Supplementary Material follows this order and provides a proof roadmap at the start of \cref{supp:pairwise_asymptotics}.

\subsection{Calibrated Candidate Expansions}\label{sec:source_inference}

We first translate the identification and calibration properties in \cref{lem:main_dr,lem:main_calibration} into sampling limits. Let $\bar\alpha_{j,a},\bar\gamma_{j,a}$ be the calibrated source population coefficients, with $\bar m_{s_j}^a=g(\phi^T\bar\alpha_{j,a})$ and $\bar q_{s_j}^a=e^{-\phi^T\bar\gamma_{j,a}}$. Let $\bar m_t^a,\bar e_t^a$ denote the calibrated target outcome and propensity limits. These limits need not equal the true functions under misspecification.

For each arm, define $\mathcal J^{a,*}=\{s_j:\operatorname{plim}\widehat\mu_{s_j}^a=\mu_t^a\}$, the set of unbiased source-assisted candidates. Membership describes agreement of population means. Inference additionally requires conditional mean transportability and one correct working model for each member, as specified in \cref{asmp:main_nuisance}.

The leading variation comes from centered site scores. The target-only score is $\varphi_t^a=\bar m_t^a+I(A=a)(Y-\bar m_t^a)/\bar e_t^a-\mu_t^a$ (\cref{supp:target_only_anchor}). For a source-assisted candidate, the target score is $\zeta_{t,s_j}^a=\bar m_{s_j}^a-\E_t\bar m_{s_j}^a$ and the source score is
\[
\xi_{t,s_j}^a=I(A=a)\bar q_{s_j}^a(Y-\bar m_{s_j}^a)
-\E_{s_j}\{I(A=a)\bar q_{s_j}^a(Y-\bar m_{s_j}^a)\}.
\]
Arguments $\boldsymbol X$ are suppressed. The first assumption controls sampling variation and the fitted losses. Constants are independent of $d,N$; full tail, overlap, and predictor-margin conditions are in \cref{asmp:pairwise_data,asmp:pairwise_overlap}.

\begin{assumption}[Design, link, and moments]\label{asmp:main_sampling}
For $a\in\{0,1\}$ and sites used by the target-only estimator and candidates in $\mathcal J^{a,*}$, there exist $C,\kappa,\delta>0$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item $D_i\mid R_i=r\stackrel{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}P_r$, $\mathcal D_r\perp\mathcal D_{r'}$ for $r\ne r'$, and $n_r/N\to\rho_r^{\mathrm{all}}>0$. Balanced fold memberships are chosen independently of the observed records, as in \cref{sec:algorithms}.
\item Almost surely $\|\phi\|_\infty\le C$, and
\[
\lambda_{\min}\!\left[\E_r\{I(A=a)\phi\phi^T\}\right]\ge\kappa,
\qquad \lambda_{\max}\{\E_r(\phi\phi^T)\}\le C.
\]
\item $g\in C^2(\mathbb R)$, $g'>0$, and, for $|u|\le2M$,
\[
C^{-1}\le g'(u)\le C,\qquad |g(u)|+|g''(u)|\le C.
\]
The conditional-mean and tail bounds, quantitative overlap, and population-predictor margins in \cref{asmp:pairwise_overlap}(b)--(d) hold. In particular, the ideal source joint weights are bounded above and away from zero.
\item For each $s_j\in\mathcal J^{a,*}$,
\[
\E_t|\varphi_t^a|^{2+\delta}\le C,\qquad
\E_t|\zeta_{t,s_j}^a|^{2+\delta}+\E_{s_j}|\xi_{t,s_j}^a|^{2+\delta}\le C.
\]
The target bound applies also when $\mathcal J^{a,*}=\varnothing$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

Positive site fractions keep pairwise and network sample sizes on the same asymptotic scale. The population design bounds allow singular empirical matrices when $d$ exceeds the local sample size. The link and tail bounds control nuisance error; the score moments support the central limit theorem.

For the paired losses in \cref{eq:gamma_calibrated_loss,eq:alpha_calibrated_loss}, set $n_{t,s_j}=n_t+n_{s_j}$. Effective sparsity $s_{\mathrm{eff},j,a}$ counts both calibrated supports and every correctly specified initial support, including intercepts. Use the population targets in \cref{asmp:pairwise_nuisance} and penalties satisfying
\[
\lambda_\alpha,\lambda_\gamma,\lambda_{\alpha,\mathrm{init}},\lambda_{\gamma,\mathrm{init}}
\asymp\sqrt{\log(2d)/n_{t,s_j}},
\]
with the sufficiently large multiplier specified in \cref{lem:calibration_rates}. That lemma also treats bounded $d$ at the same penalty order. The remaining requirements concern model correctness and sparsity.

\Needspace{5\baselineskip}
\begin{assumption}[Source models and sparsity]\label{asmp:main_nuisance}
For every $s_j\in\mathcal J^{a,*}$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item $s_j\in\mathcal S_a^*$ and $\bigl(\bar m_{s_j}^a=m_t^{a,\star}\ \text{or}\ \bar q_{s_j}^a=q_{s_j}^{a,\star}\bigr)$.
\item $s_{\mathrm{eff},j,a}\log(2d)=o(\sqrt{n_{t,s_j}})$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

A sparse true model alone need not yield a sparse calibrated limit for the misspecified model; \cref{prop:sparse_population_targets} gives sufficient conditions. Initial and calibrated coefficients remain distinct. The predictor-margin condition places calibrated population predictors and true predictors of correctly specified initial models within $[-M,M]$, leaving room before clipping at $2M$.

\Cref{lem:calibration_rates} gives coefficient errors of order $s_{\mathrm{eff},j,a}\lambda$ in $\ell_1$ and $\sqrt{s_{\mathrm{eff},j,a}}\lambda$ in $\ell_2$, where $\lambda\asymp\sqrt{\log(2d)/n_{t,s_j}}$. Calibration makes the population remainder $O_p(s_{\mathrm{eff},j,a}\lambda^2)=o_p(N^{-1/2})$, and outer cross-fitting controls the empirical error. Below, $\widehat\mu_{s_j}^a$ pools the candidate's outer-evaluation contributions.

\begin{lemma}[Calibrated source expansion]\label{thm:main_pairwise_dr_normality}
\FACEHDSourceExpansion
\end{lemma}

The two leading terms describe variation in target predictions and source residuals. Independence across sites makes their variances additive, while shared target observations induce dependence across candidates. This is the structure used in \cref{lem:main_covariance}.

The \hyperref[proof:main_source_expansion]{proof of \cref*{thm:main_pairwise_dr_normality}} is given in \cref{supp:pairwise_main_proof}. It uses the nuisance rates in \cref{lem:calibration_rates} and the second-order bounds in \cref{lem:pairwise_bias_variance}, then establishes the cross-fitted expansion and fitted-score covariance consistency. The argument covers logistic, linear, and Poisson working means under the stated predictor and tail conditions.

Aggregation also needs a valid target-only reference in each arm. With a correct target outcome or logistic propensity model and the target regularity, sparsity, and penalty conditions of \cref{prop:target_only_asymptotic_linearity}, calibrated target-only estimation satisfies
\[
\widehat\mu_t^a-\mu_t^a
=n_t^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal D_t}\varphi_{t,i}^a+o_p(N^{-1/2}),
\]
with consistent fitted-score covariances, including cross-arm covariance. Thus aggregation combines linear expansions evaluated at each candidate's calibrated limits, retaining the corresponding model-correctness conditions.

\subsection{Joint Aggregation and Source Selection}\label{sec:joint_inference}

Aggregation learns weights on the outer training observations used for calibration. \Cref{asmp:main_training_moments} collects the moment conditions required by the aggregation argument. For the prescribed fitting procedure, \cref{prop:training_moment_verification} derives these conditions from the source and target candidate conditions in \cref{sec:source_inference}.

\Needspace{6\baselineskip}
\begin{assumption}[Training means and score covariances]\label{asmp:main_training_moments}
On each outer training sample, write $n=n_{-k_1}$. The notation $\widehat\mu_r^a(\bar m,\bar e,\bar q)$ means the same candidate mean evaluated at calibrated population nuisance functions. Hatted scores replace these functions by fitted ones and center within each inner calibration fold. For each $a$:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item For $r\in\{t\}\cup\mathcal J^{a,*}$,
\[
\sqrt n\,|\widehat\mu_r^a-\widehat\mu_r^a(\bar m,\bar e,\bar q)|=o_p(1).
\]
\item For the target and each $s_j\in\mathcal J^{a,*}$, the empirical score errors satisfy
\[
\begin{gathered}
\En_{t,-k_1}(\widehat\varphi_t^a-\varphi_t^a)^2=o_p(1),\\
\En_{t,-k_1}(\widehat\zeta_{t,s_j}^a-\zeta_{t,s_j}^a)^2
+\En_{s_j,-k_1}(\widehat\xi_{t,s_j}^a-\xi_{t,s_j}^a)^2=o_p(1).
\end{gathered}
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

Part (a) makes valid training discrepancies $O_p(n^{-1/2})$; part (b) gives consistent covariance blocks, including shared-target and cross-arm terms. For the alternative with independent inner validation, \cref{lem:nested_validation} supplies the corresponding moment control.

The oracle benchmark minimizes the contrast variance over valid candidates. Let $L_\tau^*(\eta)$ be the limiting quadratic of $n\widehat V_\tau^{(-k_1)}(\eta)$ under the convergence conditions below, and define
\[
\eta^*=\arg\min_{\eta:\,\eta_{s_j}^a=0\ \mathrm{if}\ s_j\notin\mathcal J^{a,*}}L_\tau^*(\eta),
\qquad V_\tau^*=L_\tau^*(\eta^*).
\]
The oracle optimizes both arms jointly and may retain a source in only one arm. To recover it without knowing $\mathcal J^{a,*}$, the remaining candidates must be distinguishable from the target-only reference.

\begin{assumption}[Source separation and aggregation curvature]\label{asmp:main_aggregation}
Uniformly over outer training samples:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=*]
\item For all source arms, $\widehat d_{s_j}^a\to_p\bar d_{s_j}^a$, with
\[
|\bar d_{s_j}^a|\ge b_0>0\quad(s_j\notin\mathcal J^{a,*}),
\qquad \sqrt n\,\widehat s_{s_j}^a\to_p s_{s_j}^{a,*}\in(0,\infty).
\]
\item In the quadratic representation
\[
n\widehat V_\tau^{(-k_1)}(\eta)=\widehat v_n+2\widehat{\boldsymbol l}_n^T\eta+\eta^T\widehat Q_n\eta,
\]
the coefficients satisfy $(\widehat v_n,\widehat{\boldsymbol l}_n,\widehat Q_n)\to_p(v^*,\boldsymbol l^*,Q^*)$, with $\lambda_{\min}(Q^*)>0$ and $V_\tau^*>0$. The limits agree with the population score covariances on the valid subspace.
\item $c_n\to\infty$ and $c_n/\sqrt n\to0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{assumption}

Biased candidates need stable limits and nondegenerate discrepancy scales, without the valid candidates' calibrated expansions. \Cref{prop:candidate_consistency} gives sufficient conditions for this stability. Positive quadratic curvature controls the unconstrained signed weights and makes the oracle unique.

The cutoff links these conditions to the penalty in \cref{eq:agg_penalized_objective}. For a valid candidate, $\widehat t_{s_j}^a=O_p(1)$, so its penalty vanishes with probability tending to one as $c_n$ grows. For a candidate with fixed nonzero bias, $\widehat t_{s_j}^a$ is of order $\sqrt n$, so $c_n/\sqrt n\to0$ makes its penalty diverge. These two behaviors yield the following result.

\begin{theorem}[Joint treatment-effect aggregation and inference]\label{thm:main_face_hd_aggregation}

\end{theorem}

The \hyperref[proof:main_aggregation]{proof of \cref*{thm:main_face_hd_aggregation}} in \cref{supp:joint_aggregation_proof} first bounds the signed weights, then uses the optimality conditions to exclude biased arms and establish oracle-weight convergence. The $o_p(1)$ weight error then multiplies a held-out valid-candidate discrepancy of order $N^{-1/2}$, making the fitted-weight and oracle contrasts first-order equivalent. The calibrated expansions give the normal limit without an additional first-order weight-estimation term.

\subsection{Variance Estimation and Efficiency}\label{sec:theory_variance}

The limiting variance retains the site and cross-arm structure used to learn weights in \cref{sec:adaptive_aggregation}. At the oracle weights, write the centered target contribution as $G_t^a=(1-\sum_j\eta_{s_j}^{a,*})\varphi_t^a+\sum_j\eta_{s_j}^{a,*}\zeta_{t,s_j}^a$ and the source contribution as $G_{s_j}^a=\eta_{s_j}^{a,*}\xi_{t,s_j}^a$. Independence across sites gives
\[
V_\tau^*=\sum_{r\in\{t,s_1,\ldots,s_K\}}\frac{\operatorname{Var}_r(G_r^1-G_r^0)}{\rho_r^{\mathrm{all}}}.
\]
The within-site difference preserves cross-arm covariance. Whereas $V_\tau^*$ is on the $\sqrt N$ scale, the variance of $\widehat\tau_t$ is estimated using the held-out contributions from \cref{eq:main_complete_tate}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:main_outer_tate_variance}
\widehat V_\tau^{\mathrm{out}}=\sum_r\frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_r(\widehat G_{r,i}^1-\widehat G_{r,i}^0)}{n_r},
\qquad N\widehat V_\tau^{\mathrm{out}}\to_p V_\tau^*.
\end{equation}
Each empirical second central moment pools outer-evaluation contributions within its site. Variance consistency and the normal limit give the Wald interval $\widehat\tau_t\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat V_\tau^{\mathrm{out}}}$.

Because $\eta=0$ is feasible, the oracle contrast variance cannot exceed that of calibrated target-only estimation. Strict improvement requires a valid source direction that reduces this variance; compatibility alone does not ensure a gain. The comparison concerns the treatment contrast and retains cross-arm covariance, as in the joint optimization in Method.

The simulation and RHC analyses report the leading standard error $\sqrt{\widehat V_\tau^{\mathrm{out}}}$ and its Wald interval. No additional weight-learning term is included in these primary intervals. The local sensitivity of learned weights to training moments is retained separately as an optional diagnostic, holding nuisance fits, tuning values, and selected active sets fixed. \Cref{supp:reported_variance} distinguishes this diagnostic from the leading variance estimator covered by \cref{eq:main_outer_tate_variance}.

The theorem requires separated nonzero source biases and a growing cutoff; it does not establish uniform protection against biases shrinking on the sampling-error scale. The simulation study next examines precision and coverage for the implemented fixed-cutoff procedure, including weak departures from transportability.

\section{Simulation Study}\label{sec:simulations}

We examine precision and coverage for the TATE under working-model misspecification and source incompatibility. The design varies these two features separately: omitted interactions misspecify the working models, while a shift in one source treatment group violates transportability. Compatible sources provide a baseline for assessing the precision gained through borrowing.

\subsection{Data Generation and Fitted Models}
Each setting has 200 paired Monte Carlo replicates and $p=200$ covariates, of which only the first four are active. The target covariates are independent standard normals truncated at $\pm2.5$ and rescaled to variance one. Write $z_\kappa=X_1(1+\kappa X_2)/\sqrt{1+\kappa^2}$. The target treatment logit is $h_A=0.35z_{\kappa_A}-0.25X_2+0.125X_3-0.0625X_4$, and covariate shift follows $h_X=0.25z_{\kappa_A}-0.20X_2+0.15X_3-0.10X_4$. Source covariates and treatment are drawn jointly according to $f_{s_j}^\star e_{s_j}^{a,\star}\propto f_t^\star\exp\{(j/K)h_X+(a-\tfrac12)h_A\}$, normalized over covariates and treatment arms. Outcomes are Bernoulli with
\[
\operatorname{logit}P_r(Y=1\mid\boldsymbol X,A=a)
=a-\tfrac12+0.25z_{\kappa_Y}+0.20X_2+0.10X_3+0.05X_4
+a\rho I(r=s_1).
\]
At $\rho=0$, all sources are transportable. Increasing $\rho$ changes only source $s_1$'s treated-arm outcome, leaving its control arm and all other sources compatible. We consider $\rho\in\{0,0.5,1,1.5,2\}$ and $K\in\{2,4,6,8\}$ sources, with 1,000 observations per site and total sample size $N=1000(K+1)$. This design allows borrowing to differ by treatment arm. We expect compatible sources to improve precision and large source discrepancies to be easier to penalize than small discrepancies. Increasing $K$ increases the total sample size and, when $\rho>0$, decreases the fraction of sources with an incompatible arm. Numerical integration supplies the true target treatment effect.

Working nuisance models contain an intercept and all $p$ main effects but omit $X_1X_2$. Scenarios 1--3 set $(\kappa_Y,\kappa_A)$ to $(0,0)$, $(0.75,0)$, and $(0,0.75)$, respectively. At $\rho=0$ these represent both models correct, outcome misspecification only, and treatment/joint-weight misspecification only. \Cref{tab:simulation_models} summarizes which interactions are generated and omitted from the fitted models. The scenarios separate misspecification of a working model from violation of the source transportability condition.

\subsection{Implementation and Comparators}
RoCE uses two-layer ten-fold cross-fitting, one-round target outcome initialization, 100 nuisance penalty values, a numerical predictor radius of 12, and the fixed aggregation cutoff $c=2$. Its two arm-specific weight vectors are optimized jointly for the TATE. Benchmarks are calibrated target-only AIPW, sample-size (SS) and inverse-variance (IVW) aggregation of local arm means, density-ratio-weighted source AIPW combined with the target by inverse variance (Federated-DR), and density-ratio-weighted AIPW with common pooled fits (Pooled-DR) \citep{bang2005doubly,xiong2023federated}. The SS and IVW benchmarks aggregate local arm means without transport adjustment; IVW includes a nonnegative between-site variance adjustment. \Cref{supp:simulation_details} describes the roles of these comparators. The methods use the same generated data within each replicate.

We report root mean squared error (RMSE) and coverage of nominal $95\%$ TATE intervals, with pointwise Monte Carlo intervals for RoCE. These comparisons evaluate complete procedures, so they do not isolate the contribution of any single RoCE component. The fixed-cutoff interpretation is given in \cref{rem:finite_sample_wald_cutoff}.

\subsection{Results}
With compatible sources ($\rho=0$), RoCE reduces RMSE by approximately $35$--$70\%$ relative to calibrated target-only estimation and gives coverage near the nominal level across the three scenarios (\cref{fig:sim_rmse,fig:sim_coverage}). Some nonadaptive comparators have slightly lower RMSE in these settings. The precision gain from borrowing is therefore most clearly distinguished from the additional protection needed when sources become incompatible.

The precision gains at $\rho=0$ make source compatibility consequential: retaining a biased contribution can trade variance reduction for bias. Weak source deviations remain difficult to detect. At $\rho=0.5$ and $K=2$, RoCE coverage is $71.0$--$74.0\%$ across the three scenarios, and RMSE is $6.6$--$7.4\%$ higher than target-only. Undercoverage persists for larger $K$ at this deviation. These results show that finite-sample borrowing can retain enough source bias to outweigh a reduction in variance, even when one working model is correctly specified. Calibration of nuisance fits does not resolve uncertainty about source compatibility.

With larger deviations ($\rho\ge1$), RoCE generally retains precision gains and near-nominal coverage, whereas nonadaptive borrowing can incur large errors and undercoverage. Larger discrepancies make the incompatible treated arm easier to penalize while compatible arms remain available. Gains tend to increase with $K$, alongside the larger total sample size and smaller incompatible-source fraction. Taken together, the results identify weak source deviations as the difficult borrowing regime. The pattern is consistent with the separation argument in the theory, but the fixed-cutoff experiments do not establish uniform inference as source biases approach zero.

\section{All of Us One-Year Observational Application}\label{sec:realdata}
The simulations separate model misspecification from source incompatibility under known effects. In observational data, compatibility is unknown and must be considered alongside the study design. We examine one-year recorded dermatologic risk after initial exposure to a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1) or a dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor (DPP-4) among participants with type 2 diabetes. The statistical question is whether other geographic groups can improve precision for a designated target group while preserving that group's treatment contrast. The analysis uses electronic health records from the All of Us Research Program Controlled Tier version 8, available through the Researcher Workbench \citep{allofus2019,allofus2026cdr8}. Geographic groups emulate distributed sites within one program, allowing the borrowing procedure to be illustrated on a common data resource.

\subsection{Cohort and Target Contrast}
Treatment denotes initial recorded exposure to GLP-1 rather than DPP-4, with index defined by the earlier first recorded exposure. We require a type 2 diabetes diagnosis by index and exclude participants with a prior diagnosis from the prespecified composite dermatologic outcome set. Subsequent exposure to the other class does not change the initial assignment. The outcome is a recorded composite dermatologic diagnosis within one year; \cref{supp:aou_sites} provides the detailed exposure and cohort rules. \textcolor{red}{[To be completed: specify the diseases or diagnostic categories included in the composite dermatologic outcome and provide the corresponding code definitions in the supplement.]}

\textcolor{orange}{The composite includes hidradenitis suppurativa, inflammatory dermatosis, psoriasis, cutaneous lupus erythematosus, eczema, urticaria, bullous pemphigoid, pemphigus vulgaris, and alopecia areata, as represented by the selected concepts and their descendants. The concept identifiers and composite outcome definition are provided in \cref{supp:aou_sites}.}

An event recorded in the first year establishes a positive outcome; in the absence of an event, a negative outcome requires available follow-up through one year. We use the last condition-occurrence date as a follow-up proxy and exclude unclassifiable outcomes without censoring weighting. Of $15{,}339$ baseline participants with geographic information, $2{,}074$ had an unclassifiable one-year outcome, leaving $13{,}265$ evaluable participants.

The first ZIP-code digit defines Northeast (0--1), South (2, 3, 7), Midwest (4--6), and West (8--9) groups. All four meet the minimum of 25 participants per treatment arm after exclusions. We designate the largest group, Northeast, as the target for this methodological illustration ($n_t=5{,}040$): $1{,}082$ initially received GLP-1, with 90 recorded events, and $3{,}958$ received DPP-4, with 277 events. The other regions serve as sources. Their treatment-arm sizes and recorded event counts are given in \cref{tab:aou_sites}, together with the cohort-definition details in \cref{supp:aou_sites}. The target is held fixed across all methods; changing it would change the estimand.

For this evaluable target cohort, $A=1$ denotes GLP-1 and $A=0$ denotes DPP-4. The adjusted risk difference targets $\tau_t=\mu_t^1-\mu_t^0$, displayed in \cref{fig:aou_oneyear} as $100\tau_t$ percentage points. Positive values indicate higher recorded one-year risk under GLP-1.

\subsection{Adjustment and Estimation}
Models adjust for age, ethnicity, gender, race, sex at birth, and pre-index diagnosis-derived PheCode counts. Counts distinguish the year before index from earlier history; concepts defining diabetes and the dermatologic outcome are excluded. A $5\%$ prevalence screen among participants with mapped PheCodes yields 154 count features among 181 model features. This screen preceded splitting; centering and scaling use outer training samples. RoCE uses five outer folds, one-round source calibration, joint TATE aggregation, numerical predictor radii of $3$ at sources and $5$ at the target, and a fixed cutoff of $2$. The implementation uses the finite-sample tuning convention described in \cref{rem:finite_sample_wald_cutoff}; \cref{supp:reported_variance} gives the RoCE standard-error calculation.

The 181 features provide a broad adjustment set whose relevance can differ across treatment and outcome models. The geographic groups also differ in treatment composition and recorded event frequencies (\cref{tab:aou_sites}); these marginal differences motivate transport adjustment but do not establish conditional incompatibility. The same six methods used in the simulation comparison are reported. The target-only estimate supplies a within-population reference, and the borrowing estimators examine the precision available from the other geographic groups under their respective modeling and aggregation choices.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Target-specific borrowing involves two statistical decisions: how to adjust each source treatment group and how much information to borrow from it. RoCE links these decisions through a source-assisted estimating equation. A joint weight combines treatment and population adjustment, making paired high-dimensional calibration possible. The calibrated candidates then enter an aggregation problem that targets the treatment contrast and retains shared-target and cross-arm covariance. With target initialization, the required fitting tasks can be batched into one communication round.

The separation between calibration and aggregation clarifies the scope of robustness. Calibration controls the first-order effect of nuisance estimation under either correct working model, given transportability and the stated regularity. Aggregation addresses discrepancies that remain because a source outcome relationship is incompatible. Its arm-specific form can preserve useful control outcomes from a source whose treated outcomes are shifted. The oracle variance comparison formalizes the precision available from eligible contributions relative to calibrated target-only estimation; the training-moment and separation conditions are essential to that conclusion.

Weak source deviations are the principal finite-sample difficulty in the reported simulations. A small discrepancy can be hard to distinguish from sampling variation while still contributing enough bias to impair coverage after borrowing. This explains why favorable performance with compatible or clearly incompatible sources does not extend automatically to intermediate settings. The growing-cutoff theorem concerns separated biases, whereas the simulation and RHC analyses use a fixed cutoff and the leading standard error. The optional local weight-sensitivity diagnostic does not resolve source-selection bias. Inference under local source biases or unstable selection remains an open problem.

The All of Us and RHC analyses illustrate the resulting precision gains for geographic and insurance-defined target populations, respectively. The RHC leading-variance interval includes zero; the All of Us interval convention remains to be reconciled with its supplied summaries. Their substantive interpretation depends on measured confounding, exposure definition, and outcome ascertainment as well as borrowing. In All of Us, restricting to classifiable one-year outcomes makes follow-up selection part of the study population definition. Moreover, both examples emulate distributed sites within a common study; deployment across institutions also requires compatible feature definitions and an explicit accounting of preprocessing communication.

Several extensions follow from these limitations. Growing numbers of sources require simultaneous control of discrepancies and covariance estimation. Weaker sparsity conditions would accommodate broader clinical feature sets, and follow-up adjustment would extend the analysis to censored outcomes. Other effect measures, including parameters of marginal structural models, require their own transport scores and variance objectives. These extensions preserve the central distinction between fitting a reliable source contribution and deciding whether it is informative for the target population.
